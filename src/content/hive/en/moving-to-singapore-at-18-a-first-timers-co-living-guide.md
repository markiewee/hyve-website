Turning 18 changes one thing that actually matters for housing: since 2009, Singapore law gives you full contractual capacity at 18, not 21. That means you can sign your own tenancy or licence agreement in your own name, without needing a parent or guardian to co-sign it as a matter of law. Some landlords and operators still ask for a guarantor as their own business policy, especially for a first-time renter with no local track record, but the legal barrier that used to sit at 21 is gone once you turn 18.

Everything else in this guide is the practical side: banking, a SIM card, finding a room, viewing it safely, and picking housing that actually suits someone doing this for the first time, whether that's a university foundation year, the first year of a degree, or a first job.

## What changes legally at 18, in plain terms

Singapore's Civil Law (Amendment) Act 2009 lowered the age of contractual capacity from 21 to 18 for most contracts, including tenancy and licence agreements. Before that change, anyone under 21 legally needed a guardian involved. Now, once you're 18, a contract you sign has effect as if you were an adult, unless the specific contract type is excluded.

| Right | Age it starts |
|---|---|
| Signing a tenancy or licence agreement in your own name | 18, since the Civil Law (Amendment) Act 2009 lowered contractual capacity from 21 |
| Buying alcohol | 18 |
| Getting a driving licence | 18 |
| Voting | 21 |
| General age of majority for some other legal purposes | Still 21 |

Housing contracts follow the 18 threshold, not the 21 one. Some landlords and operators still ask a first-time renter for a guarantor, but that is their own business policy, not a legal requirement.

We've covered the full under-21 legal picture, including what a minor still can't do and what changes the moment you turn 21 mid-lease, in a [separate piece on under-21 renting in Singapore](/blog/under-21-renting-in-singapore-what-the-law-actually-requires). If you're moving here specifically as a boarder still under 18, that's a different situation with different rules, covered in [our guide for under-18 boarders](/blog/housing-for-international-school-boarders-under-18-in-singapore). This article assumes you're 18 or older and renting as your own legal adult.

## Before you land: the paperwork that actually matters

You don't need much sorted before you arrive, but a few things are worth doing in the right order.

**Confirm your pass first.** Whether it's a Student Pass, an In-Principle Approval letter for a foundation or degree programme, or an Employment Pass for a first job, this is the document every landlord, bank, and telco will ask for. Everything else follows from it.

**Book housing before you land, not after.** Trying to view rooms in person while sleeping in a hotel and racing a course start date is a bad position to negotiate from. Most legitimate operators, including Lazybee, let you view remotely on video call and confirm a room before you fly.

**Keep digital and physical copies of your passport, pass approval, and any acceptance letter.** You'll be asked to show these at least three times in your first week: bank, telco, and whoever you're renting from.

## Opening a bank account as a newly-18 foreigner

Once you're 18 with a valid Singapore-issued pass or an in-principle approval letter, you're generally eligible to open an account at a bank like DBS, OCBC, or UOB. You'll typically need your passport, your pass or IPA letter, and proof of a Singapore address, which is where having your room confirmed before you arrive actually helps: a signed agreement or landlord letter usually works as that address proof.

Some digital banks and fintechs offer faster remote onboarding if you want an account moving before you land, though a full Singapore bank account is still worth setting up once you're here for local transfers, salary or allowance credit, and GIRO payments.

## Getting a SIM card, and the 30-day catch

Buy a prepaid SIM the day you land, from a Singtel, StarHub, or M1 counter, all present at Changi Airport. You'll need your physical passport to register it, no photocopies.

Here's the part first-timers miss: a SIM registered on a foreign passport is only valid for 30 days under IMDA rules. After that it gets suspended, with no refund, unless you re-register it in person using a Singapore-issued IC or work/student pass. If your Student Pass or work pass takes a few weeks to process, plan to re-register your SIM once it's in hand, or you'll lose the number.

|---|---|---|
| Confirm your pass | Student Pass, an In-Principle Approval letter, or an Employment Pass | Every landlord, bank and telco asks for it. Everything else follows |
| Book housing before you land | A remote video viewing and a confirmed room | Viewing in person while sleeping in a hotel and racing a course start date is a bad position to negotiate from |
| Keep copies of your documents | Passport, pass approval, acceptance letter, digital and physical | You will show these at least three times in week one: bank, telco, landlord |
| Open a bank account | Passport, pass or IPA letter, proof of a Singapore address | A signed room agreement or landlord letter usually satisfies the address requirement, which is why confirming housing early helps |

## Viewing a room safely, on your own, for the first time

If this is your first time renting anywhere, the viewing is where most mistakes and most scams happen. A few non-negotiables:

- **Insist on a real viewing before paying anything**, in person if you're already in Singapore, or on a live video call if you're still overseas. Photos alone, especially polished ones reused across listings, are a known scam pattern.
- **Check the person showing you the room can actually access it naturally,** with their own keys or access card, not someone who "just happens to have a spare set."
- **Bring a friend if you can, or stay on a video call with someone back home during an in-person viewing.** There's no downside to it.
- **Never wire a deposit to a personal bank account with no paper trail.** Money should go to a registered company account, and the name on the account should match the name on your agreement.
- **Ask to see identification and check it against the tenancy or licence agreement before you sign anything or pay anything.**

None of this is specific to being 18. It's just that everyone doing it for the first time skips a step they'll never skip again after the first bad experience, so it's worth reading once properly before you're standing in a strange living room with someone rushing you to decide.

## Why co-living tends to suit a first move well

If this is genuinely your first time renting anywhere, not just your first time in Singapore, co-living removes a few of the harder problems a whole-unit rental would hand you on day one.

**You're not furnishing a flat from zero.** A co-living room comes furnished, with a bed, storage, and usually wifi and utilities already bundled into one number. That's one less category of costs and decisions to manage while you're also settling into a new course or job.

**You're not the only name on the lease.** A whole-unit rental usually needs one tenant to hold the master lease, chase the others for their share, and deal directly with the landlord over deposits and repairs. In a room-by-room co-living setup, that admin sits with the operator, not with you.

**You get some built-in social contact.** Moving somewhere with no existing network is genuinely hard at 18. Living alongside other tenants, even loosely, means you're not starting entirely from zero on that front either.

**The commitment is usually shorter and clearer.** Co-living terms tend to run in defined blocks rather than the standard one- or two-year lease that's common for a whole unit, which suits someone who isn't sure yet whether their course, job, or city is a long-term fit.

| | Co-living room | Whole-unit rental |
|---|---|---|
| Furnishing | Comes furnished, with wifi and utilities usually bundled into one number | Furnish from zero |
| Who holds the lease | The operator handles the admin | One tenant holds the master lease, chases the others for their share, and deals with the landlord over deposits and repairs |
| Social contact | Built in, loosely, by living alongside other tenants | Whatever you arrange yourself |
| Commitment | Defined blocks, usually shorter and clearer | A standard one or two year lease |
| Best for | Someone doing this genuinely solo, for the first time | People who already have friends to move in with |

None of this means a whole-unit share with friends is the wrong call if you already have people to move in with. It just means that for someone doing this genuinely solo and for the first time, co-living removes more friction than it adds.

## What to actually check before you sign

Whatever type of room you land on, a few things matter regardless of age:

- **A written, stampable licence or tenancy agreement.** It only holds up if it's been e-stamped with IRAS. We've covered the [difference between a licence agreement and a tenancy agreement](/blog/licence-agreement-vs-tenancy-agreement-singapore) and who's actually meant to pay the stamp duty in separate pieces.
- **A clear security deposit policy**, ideally one month's rent, refundable at move-out barring real damage. Our [security deposit guide](/blog/security-deposit-coliving-singapore) covers what's standard.
- **What's actually bundled into the rent number.** Utilities, wifi, and cleaning should be spelled out, not left as "usually included."
- **Your pass eligibility to rent at all**, which we've covered in more depth in [can foreigners rent in Singapore](/blog/can-foreigners-rent-in-singapore) if you want the full picture on which passes qualify.

## Settling in during your first few weeks

A few small things make the first month noticeably smoother. Register for a Singpass account using your FIN once your pass is issued, since it unlocks everything from paying certain bills to using government e-services. Save the number of whoever manages your room or building, not just a general enquiries line, so a real person answers if something goes wrong at 11pm on a Tuesday. And keep your original signed, stamped agreement somewhere safe, physically and as a scanned copy, since it's the document that protects you if a dispute ever comes up later.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do I need a guarantor to rent in Singapore at 18?**
Not as a matter of law. Once you're 18, you have full contractual capacity to sign a tenancy or licence agreement yourself. Some operators still ask new-to-Singapore renters with no local history for a guarantor as their own risk policy, but that's a business decision, not a legal requirement.

**Can I open a Singapore bank account before my Student Pass is approved?**
Most banks want a valid pass or at minimum an in-principle approval letter and proof of a Singapore address. A signed room agreement usually satisfies the address requirement, which is another reason to have housing confirmed early.

**Is co-living cheaper than a whole-unit rental for a first-timer?**
It depends heavily on district and room type, but a single room is almost always a smaller monthly outlay than taking on a whole unit, and it avoids the deposit and furnishing costs that come with starting a lease from scratch.
