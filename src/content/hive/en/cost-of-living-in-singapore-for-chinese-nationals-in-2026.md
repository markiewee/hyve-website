The cost of living in Singapore for Chinese nationals runs roughly two to three times higher than a major Chinese city like Shanghai or Shenzhen, even after trimming rent by taking a room instead of a full apartment. A single professional on an Employment Pass renting a room in a shared home should budget around S$1,800 to S$2,800 a month all in: rent, food, transport, and phone. A family renting a full unit and sending one child to international school can easily clear S$8,000 to S$10,000 a month once school fees are added.

The rest of this piece breaks down where those numbers come from, plus the costs a move from China specifically runs into that a generic "moving to Singapore" budget doesn't cover: pass fees, remittance, phone and payment setup, and flights home for holidays.

## How much more expensive is Singapore than a Chinese city like Shanghai?

Cost-of-living comparison data puts Shanghai at roughly 55% to 65% cheaper than Singapore overall, once housing, groceries, and daily spending are weighed together.

The gap is driven mostly by housing and land scarcity, not everyday goods. Hawker food, public transport, and mobile data in Singapore sit closer to Chinese city prices than the headline "expensive country" reputation suggests. Rent is where the real jump happens.

## What does rent actually cost, and why a room usually makes more sense first

A room in a shared home in Singapore typically runs S$600 to S$1,200 for a basic or HDB room, up to S$1,700 to S$2,500 for a master bedroom or ensuite. A studio or one-bedroom unit on your own usually starts around S$1,900 and can pass S$3,500 in a central location. For a first move, before you have a Singapore salary history or a sense of which neighbourhood suits you, a room is the faster and lower-risk option.

There's a practical reason beyond cost. Many landlords doing a private tenancy want a local guarantor, a Singapore bank statement history, or a longer track record than a first-time arrival has. A co-living licence agreement or a master-lease operator isn't underwriting you as an individual the same way, so that friction mostly disappears. It's one less thing to solve in your first week.

For the fuller breakdown of deposits, stamp duty, and what's included in rent versus billed separately, see [cost of renting in Singapore 2026](https://www.lazybee.sg/hive/cost-of-renting-singapore-2026-budget). That piece covers the general newcomer math in more depth than we'll repeat here.

## Food, groceries, and daily spending

A hawker-heavy diet runs roughly S$500 to S$1,000 a month, which is a genuine bargain by Singapore standards and not far off eating out regularly in a Chinese first-tier city once you convert currency. Groceries alone, if you cook, land closer to S$200 to S$400 a month for one person. Mixing in cafes, restaurants, and the occasional delivery order pushes the total toward S$800 to S$1,200.

Singapore's supermarkets carry a reasonable range of Chinese grocery staples, and most neighbourhoods have at least one store stocking familiar sauces, snacks, and instant noodles. It's not a like-for-like match with home, but it's not a bare cupboard either.

## Transport costs in Singapore

Public transport for a daily commute typically costs S$100 to S$150 a month on the MRT and buses, using a contactless card or SimplyGo. That's genuinely cheap relative to the rest of the budget. Add regular Grab or taxi rides, especially for late nights or heavy luggage runs, and transport can climb to S$300 to S$400 a month.

Compared to ride-hailing costs in a major Chinese city, Singapore's public transport is usually the better value, single point of comparison. The MRT network covers most of the island reliably, so you can genuinely default to it rather than treating it as a backup.

## Phone, data, and staying connected

A local SIM matters from week one, both for practical reasons (banking OTPs, delivery apps, work) and because China roaming plans get expensive fast if you rely on them past the first few days. Prepaid SIM-only plans in Singapore start from around S$7 to S$15 a month for a moderate data allowance, with heavier data plans (500GB to 1TB) running S$14 to S$25.

Setting this up on arrival, alongside opening a local bank account, is one of the first things worth doing rather than putting off. Both usually need your pass or entry stamp and a local address, which is another reason having a room booked before you land makes the first week smoother.

## Why WeChat Pay and Alipay won't fully cover you here

This catches a lot of people out. Both apps work in Singapore, but mainly for merchants set up to accept payments from Chinese bank accounts. If your Alipay or WeChat Pay is linked only to an international card, plenty of local shops, hawker stalls, and vending machines simply won't take it the way they would in China.

In practice, you'll want a Singapore bank account, a local debit or credit card, and PayNow (Singapore's bank-to-bank instant transfer system) set up fairly early. Alipay and WeChat Pay stay useful for calling home or paying Chinese merchants online, but they're not a substitute for local payment rails once you're actually living here.

## Sending money home and remittance costs

Bank wires from Singapore to China typically carry a transfer fee of S$10 to S$50 plus an exchange rate margin around 3% above the mid-market rate if you don't check it. Digital transfer services often work out cheaper and faster, since several route through local Chinese payment rails rather than a traditional wire. Compare a couple of providers rather than defaulting to your bank's rate, the fee difference on a regular monthly transfer adds up over a year.

## Employment Pass and other pass costs

Pass eligibility and fees apply the same way regardless of nationality, there's no separate fee schedule for Chinese applicants. As of 2026, the standard Employment Pass costs S$105 for the application and a further S$225 if approved, so budget roughly S$330 total per pass, on top of whatever an employment agent charges if one's involved. Minimum qualifying salary for an EP starts around S$5,600 a month in most sectors, higher in financial services and for older applicants.

One thing that specifically applies to some China-issued degrees is a degree authentication step during the application, which can add processing time.. Build in extra lead time for this rather than assuming a fast turnaround.

## Flights home and visiting during holidays

Direct one-way flights from Singapore to Shanghai run from roughly S$106 to S$144 on a good fare, and to Beijing from around S$186. Round-trip fares over Chinese New Year or National Day cost more than off-peak dates, so booking a few months ahead for major holiday windows pays off. Budgeting one or two trips home a year, even at the higher end, is a manageable line item next to rent.

## If you're bringing family: school costs are the real budget swing

For a single professional, school fees don't apply and the numbers above hold. For a family moving with a child, international school tuition is the single biggest additional cost, running roughly S$24,000 to S$59,000 a year depending on the school, on top of application fees, a one-time registration fee, and extras like school bus transport, uniforms, and exam fees. Established schools at the upper end of that range sit well above the entry-level options.

This is worth pricing out early and separately from your household budget, since it can double a family's total monthly spend on its own.

## Frequently asked questions

**Is Singapore much more expensive than China for daily living?**
Yes, on housing especially. Cost comparison data puts Shanghai at roughly 55% to 65% cheaper than Singapore overall. Daily costs like hawker food and public transport are closer between the two than the housing gap suggests.

**Can Chinese nationals use Alipay or WeChat Pay in Singapore?**
Both apps work in Singapore, but mainly for merchants set up for Chinese bank account payments. If yours is linked only to an international card, many local shops won't accept it. Get a Singapore bank account and PayNow set up early.

**What does an Employment Pass cost in 2026?**
S$105 for the application and S$225 if approved, roughly S$330 total, plus any agent fees. The minimum qualifying salary starts around S$5,600 a month in most sectors. Fees and eligibility are the same regardless of nationality.

**Is it cheaper to rent a room or a whole apartment when relocating to Singapore?**
A room is significantly cheaper, and it's also the easier option for a first move since it usually doesn't require a local guarantor or a Singapore rental history. For the full deposit and stamp duty breakdown, see [cost of renting in Singapore 2026](https://www.lazybee.sg/hive/cost-of-renting-singapore-2026-budget).

**How much should I budget for sending money home or to Singapore before I move?**
Bank wires typically cost S$10 to S$50 plus a currency margin around 3%. Digital transfer services are often cheaper for routine remittances. Compare a couple of providers rather than defaulting to your bank's standard rate.
