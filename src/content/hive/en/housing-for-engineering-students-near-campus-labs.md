Engineering students at NUS and NTU should weight campus proximity more heavily than most other students, because so much of the degree happens in a lab or workshop, not a lecture hall. NUS's engineering programmes sit within the College of Design and Engineering (CDE) on the Kent Ridge campus, and NTU's sit within the College of Engineering on the main Nanyang campus in Jurong West. Both put lab and project work on a schedule the timetable doesn't control, which is exactly why a short commute matters more here than it does for a course that's mostly seminars and readings.

This guide covers why that's true specifically for engineering, not students in general, and where that leaves you if you're choosing a room near either campus.

## Why Engineering Is Different From a Lecture-Heavy Degree

A lot of degrees run entirely on a published timetable: class starts, class ends, you go home. Engineering doesn't work that way for long stretches of the course. Lab modules, project teams, and specialised equipment mean the actual work often continues after the timetabled session is over, and it isn't always something you can pick back up from a laptop at home.

NUS's Faculty of Engineering, now folded into CDE alongside the School of Design and Environment since 2021, was established in 1968 and is the largest faculty in the university ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/National_University_of_Singapore)). NTU's College of Engineering is NTU's largest academic division, with a student population of more than 10,500 undergraduates and 3,500 graduate students across five schools ([Wikipedia](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Nanyang_Technological_University)). At both universities, engineering isn't a small corner of campus. It's the biggest single group of students working through lab-based coursework at any given time, which is part of why lab and workshop capacity gets tight exactly when everyone needs it most, around deadlines.

## Where NUS's Engineering Facilities Actually Are

CDE, and the engineering departments within it, sit on the Kent Ridge campus, with a listed address of 9 Engineering Drive 1 ([NUS CDE](https://cde.nus.edu.sg/)). That places engineering teaching and lab space within the main Kent Ridge grounds, reachable the same way as the rest of campus: Kent Ridge MRT (CC24) on the Circle Line, with one-north and Buona Vista the next two stops out, all connected by NUS's free internal shuttle.

## Why Lab and Workshop Access Outside Class Hours Matters

Three things push engineering students toward late or irregular lab time in a way a purely lecture-based course doesn't.

**Project deadlines that don't fit a class slot.** Coursework in engineering modules is often built around a project or a piece of lab work due on a fixed date, not a weekly reading. When a deliverable is due, the work compresses into whatever time is left before it, which routinely spills past the end of the scheduled lab session.

**Equipment and workshop access you can't replicate at home.** Testing rigs, fabrication tools, specialised software licensed to lab machines, and shared equipment booked by multiple project teams all mean the actual work has to happen on campus, in a specific room, not wherever you happen to be. If that room's booking runs late or the queue for shared equipment is long, the trip home and back isn't a small cost.

**Final year project (FYP) crunch.** FYP is the point where this compounds hardest. It's a longer, self-directed piece of work that often needs sustained lab or workshop time as a submission date closes in, on a schedule set by the project itself and your supervisor's availability, not by a fixed timetable. Exact after-hours access policies vary by department and semester, so check with your own school before assuming a lab is open whenever you need it. What's consistent across schools is that FYP terms are when students most often find themselves finishing something on campus late, then needing to actually get home.

None of this is unique to engineering as a category of "odd hours," and the general playbook for living well on an irregular lab or clinical schedule, shift patterns, sleep, noise, food access, applies whether you're in engineering, architecture, or a clinical programme. ## What to Check Before You Book a Room Near an Engineering Campus

- Confirm which faculty building or lab cluster you'll actually be using most, since a room that's close to one part of a large campus can still be a long walk from a specific engineering block.
- Ask your department or seniors what after-hours lab and workshop access actually looks like for your programme, rather than assuming a blanket policy.
- Time your commute for a late-night return, not just the daytime trip, since bus and shuttle frequency usually drops sharply after normal class hours.
- If you're heading into FYP year, weigh proximity more heavily than you would in earlier years, since that's when the odd-hours pattern tends to be most frequent.
- Check what's actually included in the room, furnishing, wifi, utilities, so a late finish on campus doesn't turn into more admin once you get home.

## FAQ

**Do engineering students need to live closer to campus than other students?**
It helps more than it does for a lecture-heavy course, because engineering coursework, lab modules, and FYP work often run past scheduled class hours and depend on equipment you can only access on campus.

**Where are NUS's engineering faculties located?**
Within the College of Design and Engineering (CDE) on the Kent Ridge campus, at 9 Engineering Drive 1. It's reachable via Kent Ridge MRT (CC24) on the Circle Line, or one-north and Buona Vista, all connected by NUS's free shuttle.

**Where is NTU's College of Engineering located?**
On NTU's main campus in Jurong West, the same campus served by Pioneer and Boon Lay MRT stations and NTU's internal shuttle network.

**Is NTU's College of Engineering really its largest faculty?**
Yes. It's NTU's largest academic division, with a student population of more than 10,500 undergraduates and 3,500 graduate students across five schools.

**What's different about FYP year specifically?**
Final year project work is self-directed and often needs sustained lab or workshop time as the submission date approaches, on a schedule set by the project rather than the timetable, which is when a short commute matters most.
