If you're working a full-time job and doing a part-time degree in the evenings or on weekends, the housing that suits you best usually isn't student housing at all. What you actually need is a stable, quiet private room with a lease that runs as long as your course does, ideally shared with other working adults rather than a rotating cast of undergrads. Co-living fits that brief better than most people expect, since it's built around private rooms and predictable terms rather than dorm-style living.

That's the short version. The rest of this covers what part-time degree study in Singapore actually looks like right now, who's doing it, and what to weigh up when you're choosing where to live around a job and a class schedule at the same time.

## Who Is Actually Doing This

Part-time degrees in Singapore aren't a niche path. The Singapore University of Social Sciences (SUSS) alone runs more than 80 undergraduate and graduate programmes across full-time and part-time modes, most of them built specifically for people already in the workforce. Entry criteria usually ask for two years of full-time work experience, or current full-time employment, on top of the usual academic qualifications. That's a deliberate design choice, not an afterthought bolted onto a full-time programme.

NTU and NUS run the same idea through different channels. NTU offers part-time Bachelor of Engineering routes for polytechnic diploma holders who want to keep working while they study, plus SkillsFuture Work-Study degrees like the BTech in Computing, aimed at professionals adding a credential without stepping out of their job. NUS runs a similar lane through its School of Continuing and Lifelong Education (SCALE), offering part-time BTech programmes such as Supply Chain Management for polytechnic graduates who are currently employed. Age isn't really the sorting variable here, employment status and stage of career are. Someone in their late 20s finishing a diploma-to-degree pathway and someone in their 40s doing a mid-career pivot both land in the same part-time cohort.

## What Makes This Different From a Full-Time Course

A full-time undergraduate's housing decision is built around one thing: distance to campus, because that's most of their week. A part-time student working full-time is optimising for something else entirely: a schedule that has two anchors, an office and a classroom, on different days, at different times, usually with evening or weekend classes squeezed around a 9-to-6.

That changes what "good housing" means. Classes running two or three evenings a week means getting home, changing, and getting back out again, so a commute that works from the office matters as much as one that works from home. A course that runs three to six years, which is normal for a part-time degree, means the lease question isn't "how do I cover one semester" but "how do I not have to move house twice while I'm still finishing my degree." And coming home after a 10pm class to prep for a client call the next morning means quiet, working housemates matter more than a lively house full of first-years.

## Where the Campuses Actually Are

SUSS runs its main campus at 463 Clementi Road, reachable via King Albert Park MRT on the Downtown Line, with a new Rochor campus announced but not yet operational. NUS SCALE's part-time programmes run out of multiple sites depending on the course, including locations closer to the west and central parts of the island. NTU's part-time engineering and work-study routes are based at the main Jurong West campus, and SMU's postgraduate and executive routes sit in the Bras Basah / Bugis area in the city centre.

None of that maps onto a single "student area" the way full-time undergrad housing does. A room that works for someone doing SUSS classes at Clementi on a Tuesday evening needs to also work for their day job, which could be anywhere on the island. That's the actual planning problem, and it's a commute-times-two problem, not a campus-adjacent one.

## What This Segment Should Actually Look For in a Room

A few things matter more here than they do for a typical student rental:

- **A lease length that matches your actual timeline**, not a default 12-month term you have to keep renewing and hoping nothing changes mid-course. A multi-year part-time degree benefits from a stable base you're not re-negotiating every year.
- **Housemates who are also working adults.** A house full of people on 9-to-6 schedules, coming home tired on a Tuesday and wanting quiet, functions very differently to a house full of undergrads on exam-week or party-weekend rhythms.
- **A commute that works both ways**, from the office and from the classroom, not just from one campus gate.
- **A room that's actually set up to work and study in**, with desk space and reliable wifi, since evening study sessions after a full day at the office are the norm, not the exception.
- **An all-in rent** that folds in utilities and furnishing, so a multi-year budget stays predictable instead of shifting with every bill.
- **No dorm-style compromises.** Shared bunks, curfews, or a hall structured around an academic calendar aren't built for someone who also has a 9am meeting the next day.

## Why Co-Living Tends to Fit This Better Than the Alternatives

The two default options most people consider are a traditional shared HDB or condo room found through a private landlord, or staying with family longer than they'd like to. Both have real drawbacks for this specific situation. A private landlord rental usually means furnishing the room yourself, chasing a landlord for repairs, and negotiating lease length one listing at a time, none of which is a good use of time when you're already stretched between a job and a degree. Staying with family for years longer than planned, purely to avoid the hassle of renting, is a real and common compromise, and one that a part-time student working full-time shouldn't have to default to just because renting felt like too much admin on top of everything else.

Co-living sits between those two, and for this segment specifically, it tends to close the gap. A private room comes fully furnished and move-in ready, with wifi and utilities already bundled into the rent, so there's no separate admin trail to manage around class nights. Lease terms are published on the listing itself rather than negotiated down over weeks, which matters when your available time for house-hunting is squeezed into the same evenings your classes are competing for. And because co-living houses tend to draw a mix of working professionals rather than exclusively fresh graduates or full-time undergrads, the household rhythm is closer to what someone balancing a job and a degree actually needs.

## A Few Common Questions

**Is co-living only for younger renters?**
No. Co-living houses in Singapore generally draw a mixed household of working professionals across a range of ages and life stages, not a single age bracket. What matters more than age is finding a house where the household rhythm, quiet evenings, working schedules, actually matches yours.

**Can I get a lease that matches a multi-year part-time degree?**
Terms vary by operator and by room, so check the specific listing rather than assuming either way. The point of looking at co-living specifically is that the term on the listing tends to be the real, checkable term, not an opening position for negotiation.

**Do I need to be near my university to make this work?**
Less than you'd think, since the actual constraint is two commutes, not one. A location with strong MRT access to the wider island tends to serve this schedule better than one picked purely for proximity to a single campus.

**What if my work schedule changes and I need to move?**
This is exactly why a lease that isn't locked to a full year by default is worth prioritising when your job and your course schedule can both shift over a multi-year programme. Check the terms on any listing before signing.

**Is furnished housing worth paying for if I'm only home a few hours a day?**
For someone already stretched between a job and evening classes, not having to source, buy, and later dispose of furniture is time and admin saved, which tends to matter more the busier the schedule is.
