For a Thai national moving to Singapore in 2026, a realistic all-in monthly budget lands between S$1,800 and S$3,000 for someone renting a room, or S$3,200 and S$5,000 for someone renting a whole unit alone. That covers rent, food, transport, phone and wifi, and everyday spending. On top of the monthly number, you'll need landing cash for deposits, advance rent, and pass costs before your first Singapore paycheck clears, and it helps to plan around a baht that has weakened against the Singapore dollar over the past year.

This piece breaks down what that budget actually looks like, the visa and pass routes that apply to Thai citizens specifically, and what's different about moving here from Thailand compared with moving from elsewhere in the region.

## How much does it cost to live in Singapore as a Thai national?

Singapore is significantly more expensive than Bangkok by any measure. Numbeo's 2026 comparison data puts it starkly: you'd need roughly S$4,700 (about ฿120,000) in Bangkok to match the standard of living that S$12,000 buys in Singapore, assuming you rent in both cities. Housing is the single biggest gap: a comparable apartment in Bangkok runs at roughly a quarter to a third of the equivalent in central Singapore. Groceries and mid-range dining are also materially cheaper back home.

The core monthly numbers in Singapore, all-in:

- Room in a shared or co-living home: roughly S$1,800 to S$3,000 a month, covering rent, food, transport, and basics
- Whole studio or one-bedroom unit alone: roughly S$3,200 to S$5,000 a month
- A single worker on a modest budget, room plus daily living, typically lands toward the lower end of the room range, closer to S$1,600 to S$2,200, depending on lifestyle and whether meals are self-cooked or hawker-based

## What visa or pass do Thai citizens actually need?

Getting this right before you commit to a budget matters, since your pass type shapes both your legal ability to sign a lease and your timeline for moving.

**Short visits.** Thai passport holders can enter Singapore visa-free for stays of up to 30 days, with no advance visa application needed. That covers a viewing trip or a short exploratory visit, but it is not permission to work, and overstaying or working on a visa-free entry is a real risk, not a technicality.

**Working here.** For employment, the pass depends on the role and salary, not nationality. As of 2026, the Employment Pass requires a minimum fixed monthly salary of S$5,600 (S$6,200 in financial services), rising with age toward S$10,700+ at 45, plus a passing score of 40 points under the COMPASS framework, which weighs salary, qualifications, workforce diversity, and local hiring. The S Pass, for mid-skilled roles, currently requires a minimum of S$3,300 (S$3,800 in financial services), also benchmarked upward by age. Both floors rise again from 1 January 2027, to S$6,000/S$6,600 for the EP and S$3,600/S$4,000 for the S Pass, so factor that timeline in if you're planning a move that straddles the change.

**Studying here.** Thai students are a smaller but steady presence at Singapore's universities and polytechnics. International student fees for ASEAN nationals (which includes Thailand) at NUS and NTU generally run from around S$20,000 to S$55,000 a year, with the government's Tuition Grant Scheme cutting that cost in exchange for a post-graduation work bond, usually three years, worth weighing against your longer-term plans.

Whichever route applies to you, confirm your exact pass status with ICA or MOM before signing anything, since the pass determines your ability to lease, open certain accounts, and stay long-term.

## What's different about the money side, coming from Thailand?

**The baht has weakened against the Singapore dollar.** Through 2026, THB/SGD has traded roughly between 0.0382 and 0.0414, meaning one Singapore dollar buys around 24 to 26 baht, with the rate down close to 6% over the year.. That cuts two ways: your baht savings stretch less on arrival, but a Singapore-dollar salary converts to noticeably more baht when you send money home. Use a real transfer service such as Wise or a bank wire rather than converting cash informally, and check the live rate before moving anything meaningful.

**There's a tax treaty covering the move.** Singapore and Thailand have had a double taxation agreement in force since January 2017, covering employment income among other categories, so income already taxed in one country generally isn't taxed again on the same amount in the other. Non-residents (broadly, under 183 days in Singapore in the year) pay a flat 15% on employment income or the resident progressive rate, whichever is higher; past 183 days you move onto the progressive scale instead, usually lower. Confirm your exact residency status with IRAS once you know your dates, since the threshold is exact and easy to miscount if you arrive mid-year.

**Remittance is routine, not exceptional.** Sending part of a Singapore salary home is common enough in the Thai community that it's built into most people's monthly budget as a fixed line item. Golden Mile Complex on Beach Road, long known locally as Singapore's Thai enclave, has hosted remittance counters alongside its restaurants and grocery stores since the 1980s, a practical option as well as a familiar one if you'd rather handle it in person than online.

## Where does the Thai community actually live and eat?

Golden Mile Complex is the real hub. Decades of Thai restaurants, grocery stores, tour agencies, and community services have clustered there, earning it the nickname "Little Thailand," and it remains the first stop for Thai groceries, food, and a sense of home base for many newcomers, whatever part of the island they actually live in.

You don't need to live near Golden Mile Complex to use it. It's a short MRT or bus ride from most of the island, so your choice of neighbourhood can be driven by commute, budget, and room type rather than proximity to any one enclave, a real advantage over cities where the community hub and the affordable housing aren't in the same part of town.

The Thai community in Singapore spans a wide range of work, from hospitality and skilled trades to a growing base of professionals and students, and lives spread across the island rather than clustered in one district beyond that shared social and food hub.

## A realistic first-month budget, landing from Thailand

Move-in cash needed before your first Singapore paycheck typically covers:

1. **Deposit and advance rent.** Usually one to two months' rent as deposit plus one month in advance, so two to three months' rent in cash before you get the keys. See the [full move-in cost breakdown](https://www.lazybee.sg/hive/cost-of-renting-singapore-2026-budget) for exact ranges by room type.
2. **Currency conversion buffer.** With THB trending weaker against SGD through 2026, build in a small buffer against further movement between when you plan your budget and when you actually convert and transfer funds.
3. **Pass or visa costs.** Employment Pass and S Pass application and issuance fees are typically paid by the employer, but confirm this with your employer before assuming it, since practice varies by company. Student pass costs are usually the student's own responsibility.
4. **One to two months of living costs as a buffer.** Your first local paycheck can land four to six weeks after you start, and landing without a runway is the single most common mistake new arrivals make, regardless of where they're moving from.
5. **A local SIM and bank account in week one.** Both reduce the overseas transaction fees that otherwise quietly eat into a tight first-month budget.

An all-inclusive room, where rent already bundles utilities, wifi, and cleaning into one number, genuinely simplifies this arithmetic for anyone budgeting from a currency as different from Singapore's as the baht. Instead of estimating five separate Singapore-dollar bills, you're planning around one number, a real reduction in risk when you're arriving without a local reference point.

## Frequently asked questions

**How much money do I need to move to Singapore from Thailand?**
Budget two to three months' rent in cash for deposit and advance rent, plus one to two months of living costs as a buffer before your first Singapore paycheck lands. For a room-based budget, that's typically S$4,000 to S$7,000 in total landing cash, though the exact figure depends on the room and your personal buffer preference.

**Do Thai citizens need a visa to enter Singapore?**
Short visits are visa-free for up to 30 days with no advance application needed, but working or studying here requires the correct pass (Employment Pass, S Pass, or a student pass tied to your enrolment), which is separate from tourist entry. Confirm your exact entry terms with ICA before booking travel tied to a work or study start date.

**Is it cheaper to rent a room or a whole unit in Singapore?**
A room is cheaper on nearly every measure, generally landing in the S$1,800 to S$3,000 all-in monthly range compared with S$3,200 to S$5,000 for a whole unit alone. A room, especially an all-inclusive one, is also the simpler budget to plan from a foreign currency and no local track record.

**Will I be taxed twice, once in Thailand and once in Singapore?**
No. The Singapore-Thailand double taxation agreement, in force since January 2017, means income already taxed in one country generally isn't taxed again on the same amount in the other. Your exact Singapore tax rate depends on your residency status, which is based on days spent in Singapore in the calendar year.

**Where do Thai nationals typically live in Singapore?**
There's no single residential enclave, but Golden Mile Complex on Beach Road, known locally as Little Thailand, is the established hub for Thai food, groceries, and remittance services. Housing choice is generally driven by commute and budget rather than proximity to that hub, since it's reachable from most of the island.
