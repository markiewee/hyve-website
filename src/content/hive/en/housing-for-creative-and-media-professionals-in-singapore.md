Creative and media professionals in Singapore work across a few distinct clusters, mainly one-north's Mediapolis for broadcast, gaming, and production, and the CBD and city fringe for advertising, branding, and design agencies. The bigger housing issue isn't location though. This industry runs on project contracts and freelance income, which fits badly with a standard 12-month lease built around a fixed monthly salary from day one. This piece covers both: where the work is, and why the housing shape needs to match how the income arrives.

## Where does Singapore's creative and media industry actually cluster?

There's no single official "creative district" the way biomedical research has Biopolis. The industry sits across three distinct pockets, and where you work depends heavily on which part of the industry you're in.

**One-north, and specifically Mediapolis within it,** is the closest thing to a dedicated media hub. Mediapolis is a purpose-built precinct with soundstages, green-screen facilities, post-production suites, and games and animation studios. Mediacorp, Singapore's national broadcaster, relocated its entire operation to an 800,000 sq ft campus there in 2015. Razer runs its Southeast Asia headquarters out of one-north, and Grab's headquarters sits in the same precinct. If your work is broadcast, gaming, animation, or production-adjacent, one-north is where the infrastructure is built for it.

**The CBD and nearby fringe areas** are where advertising, branding, and communications agencies tend to operate, alongside the corporate marketing teams that brief them. This is less a single street and more a scatter across Raffles Place, Robertson Quay, and River Valley, wherever an agency can get office space near the clients it services.

**The Bras Basah.Bugis precinct** is Singapore's officially branded arts, culture, and learning district, home to LASALLE College of the Arts and NAFA. It's more education and gallery-facing than commercial studio space, but it's the area where a lot of design and fine arts graduates start their networks and first freelance gigs before moving into agency or studio work elsewhere.

The practical upshot: a creative or media career here can genuinely mean working across two or three of these areas within the same year, sometimes the same month, depending on which project you're on.

## Does the Employment Pass work differently for creative roles?

No. This is worth being direct about, since it's a common assumption. The Ministry of Manpower does not run a separate salary threshold for creative, media, or arts occupations. Employment Pass applicants fall under one of two sector categories: financial services, or everything else.

For the general sector (which covers creative and media roles), the qualifying salary starts at $5,600 a month for candidates aged 23 or under, rising progressively to $10,700 a month for candidates 45 and above. These are the current rates ahead of a scheduled increase from 1 January 2027. Financial services roles sit on a higher scale, starting at $6,200 and rising to $11,800.

On top of the salary floor, most applicants also need to clear COMPASS, a points-based framework assessing salary, qualifications, workforce diversity, local hiring support, and (where relevant) a skills shortage bonus. A minimum of 40 points across the six criteria is required. Candidates earning $22,500 a month or more, or coming in for a role of one month or less, are exempted from COMPASS entirely.

## Why creative and media work runs on project income, not a fixed monthly salary

A large share of work in advertising, production, and design happens on a project or contract basis rather than permanent headcount. Agencies staff up for a pitch or campaign and staff down after it ships. Production houses hire crew per shoot. Freelance designers, editors, and copywriters bill per project, not per month.

Even professionals in a permanent role often supplement it with freelance work, or move between a permanent contract and a string of project contracts over a two or three year stretch. That's normal here in a way it isn't in banking or the public sector.

The practical effect on your finances is real. Income can be lumpy: a strong month after a project wraps, then a leaner stretch while the next brief comes in. That's manageable day to day. It becomes a problem the moment a landlord wants proof of a stable monthly salary and a year-long signature before you've even started the job.

## Why a 12-month lease is the wrong shape for this kind of income

A standard tenancy agreement in Singapore assumes two things: a tenant who's staying exactly 12 months, and a tenant with a predictable paycheck to show for it. Both assumptions break down fast for a project-based creative career.

If your next contract runs three months and then who knows, locking into a year-long lease means either paying rent on a room you've left, or scrambling to break it early, which usually costs a chunk of your deposit. If you're between contracts and can't show three months of payslips from a new employer, some landlords won't rent to you at all, regardless of your actual earning history.

Flexible, shorter-commitment housing sidesteps both problems. A room you can hold for a few months, renew if the next contract lands, or walk away from cleanly if it doesn't, matches how this industry pays people. It's not about being unable to commit. It's the housing commitment matching the employment commitment, rather than outrunning it.

## What to look for in a room as a creative or media professional

- A commute that works across more than one likely work location, since your next project could be in one-north, the CBD, or elsewhere
- A lease structure that doesn't require a year-long commitment or continuous payslip history
- All-in rent, so utilities and wifi aren't separate bills to chase in a lean month
- Reliable, fast wifi for uploading large files, video, or renders on a deadline
- A private, lockable room so gear or client materials aren't sitting in a shared space
- A household that doesn't assume a fixed 9-to-5, since shoot days and deadline crunches don't run on office hours

## Frequently asked questions

**Do creative and media professionals need a different type of work pass in Singapore?**
No. Most professional and managerial creative roles, art directors, producers, designers, editors, use the standard Employment Pass, assessed against the same general-sector salary floor and COMPASS points test as any other industry. There's no separate creative-sector visa category.

**Is it harder to rent in Singapore on freelance or project-based income?**
It can be, mainly because standard tenancy agreements assume a fixed monthly salary and a full year's commitment. Some landlords want continuous payslips before they'll sign. Flexible housing with shorter commitment periods avoids that requirement.

**Where should I live if I work across one-north and the CBD?**
There's no single perfect answer, since the two areas sit on opposite sides of the island's transport network. The right call depends on which location your current contract is anchored to and how much that's likely to shift over your next few projects.

**Can I find a room in Singapore without a 12-month lease?**
Yes. Co-living operators, including Lazybee, offer shorter, more flexible commitment periods than a standard tenancy agreement, which suits project-based income better than a fixed year-long lease.
