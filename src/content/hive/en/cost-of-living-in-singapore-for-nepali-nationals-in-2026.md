The cost of living in Singapore for a Nepali professional or student in 2026 typically runs S$1,500 to S$1,900 a month for everyday costs, before rent. Add a room in a shared home, usually S$800 to S$1,800 depending on room type and location, and a realistic all-in monthly budget lands between S$2,300 and S$3,700. That figure covers food, transport, phone and wifi, and remittance costs, but not the rent for a whole unit, which runs considerably higher.

This piece is written for Nepali nationals renting in the open market: professionals on an Employment Pass or S Pass, and students. It's worth saying upfront that this isn't the whole picture of Nepalis in Singapore. A meaningful share are Work Permit holders in construction, marine, and F&B roles, where the employer arranges housing directly under Ministry of Manpower rules, and a further group are Gurkha Contingent personnel and their families, housed through the arrangement that brought them here. If either applies to you, the budget below won't apply the same way, since housing sits outside your own monthly rent line.

## What does it cost to live in Singapore as a Nepali national in 2026?

Singapore's general cost of living for a single person, excluding rent, runs roughly S$1,500 to S$1,900 a month, covering groceries, transport, phone, and everyday spending. That's a general baseline, not built around any one nationality.

For a Nepali national specifically, two things move the number. Nepali staples like specific dals, mustard oil, and certain spice blends cost more here than at home, though far less than most newcomers expect once they find the right shops. Remittance home is also a real recurring cost that a generic cost-of-living figure leaves out entirely.

Nepalis in Singapore span a wide range: a long-standing Gurkha community whose housing and pay sit outside the open rental market, Work Permit holders in CMP and hospitality sectors whose employer houses them, and a smaller but growing number of professionals and students who rent like anyone else. Little India, especially around Rowell Road near Mustafa Centre, is where the community's restaurants and informal meeting points are concentrated, a convenient anchor point regardless of which pass you're on.

## Rent: the single biggest line item

Rent dominates the monthly number more than anything else. A room in a shared home typically runs S$800 to S$1,800 a month depending on room type, with all-inclusive co-living rooms bundling utilities and wifi into that one figure. A whole studio or one-bedroom unit costs considerably more and almost never includes utilities in the headline rent.

## Nepali food and groceries: what it actually costs

Cooking with Nepali staples in Singapore costs more than at home, but the shops around Little India and Mustafa Centre make it manageable. A monthly grocery habit built around rice, dal, mustard oil, and specific spices typically runs S$150 to S$300 a month for a single person cooking regularly.

Little India, particularly around Rowell Road, has a handful of Nepali and Himalayan restaurants where momo, dal bhat, and thukpa are on the menu, including Gurkha Palace, Everest Kitchen, and Amber Tandoor. Mustafa Centre, open 24 hours, carries a wide range of South Asian groceries that covers most of the Nepali pantry.

Eating out doesn't have to be expensive. A hawker meal, including halal stalls in most hawker centres, runs S$5 to S$10, and a plate of momo at one of the Little India Nepali restaurants runs roughly S$9 to S$12. Mixing hawker meals with home cooking, a realistic monthly food budget is S$350 to S$600 for someone cooking often, or S$650 to S$950 for someone eating out more.

## Work passes: what they mean for the budget

Most Nepali professionals moving to Singapore for skilled roles arrive on an Employment Pass or S Pass, and the pass type shapes the budget indirectly through the minimum qualifying salary tied to it. As of 2026, the Employment Pass requires a minimum qualifying salary of S$5,600 a month for general sectors and S$6,200 for financial services, and the S Pass requires S$3,300 for new applications in general sectors, both rising with age.

These are floors set by MOM, not typical offer levels, so most incoming professionals land above them.

## Sending money back to Nepal: remittance costs

Sending money home is a genuine recurring cost that generic cost-of-living guides skip entirely. The SGD to NPR corridor has a wide range of providers, and the gap between the cheapest and most expensive option is large enough to matter every month.

Digital-first providers like Wise price close to the mid-market exchange rate, which recently sat around 110 to 113 NPR per SGD, with a transparent, upfront fee rather than a hidden margin buried in the rate. Traditional bank wire transfers and some cash-pickup counters carry a much wider spread on the exchange rate, which is where most of the real cost hides.

Little India has remittance counters that Nepali workers use on their common rest day, alongside apps that let you send from your phone without queueing.

## Flights and staying connected to family

Round-trip flights between Singapore and Kathmandu vary widely by source and season, with fare trackers showing figures from roughly S$220 up to S$500 or more depending on airline, booking lead time, and whether it's a direct or connecting route.

Budgeting one or two trips home a year, plus an occasional emergency fare, is a line item most generic Singapore cost-of-living guides leave out.

## Where Nepali professionals and students tend to live

Little India, around Rowell Road and Mustafa Centre, is the obvious first answer for community, food, and remittance access. For many working professionals, though, it's not the most practical base once commute to the CBD or a business park is factored in.

Co-living rooms across the island put you within reach of Little India without requiring you to live there specifically, since MRT access makes a 15 to 20 minute trip routine. ## A realistic monthly budget for a Nepali professional in Singapore

| Item | Typical monthly cost |
|---|---|
| Room rent (shared home, often all-inclusive) | S$800 to S$1,800 |
| Food, including Nepali groceries | S$350 to S$600 |
| Transport (public transport, daily commute) | S$100 to S$150 |
| Phone and wifi (where not bundled into rent) | S$30 to S$60 |
| Remittance to Nepal (fee and spread only, not the transfer amount itself) | S$10 to S$40 |
| Flights home (averaged monthly, 1 to 2 trips a year) | S$35 to S$85 |
| **Total, room + living costs** | **S$1,325 to S$2,735** |

This is a working professional's baseline, not a hard ceiling. Renting a whole unit alone instead of a room adds roughly S$1,000 to S$2,500 more to the rent line, since a full apartment costs considerably more and rarely includes utilities.

## Myth: Nepali groceries make Singapore unaffordable

This gets said a lot, and it's overstated. Specific imported ingredients cost more here than at home, but once you know Little India and the shops around Mustafa Centre, that's one manageable line in a monthly budget, not the whole budget.

The bigger cost driver by far is rent, the same as for every nationality moving to Singapore. Anyone comparing headline "cost of living" figures against Nepal should compare rent-adjusted numbers, not grocery receipts, since rent is where the real gap sits.
