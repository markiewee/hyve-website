Moving out at 22 with a first paycheck coming in works best when you follow a simple rule: keep rent to around 30% of your net monthly income, run through a signing checklist before you commit to anything, and time your move around your job's probation period, not around move-in excitement. This guide walks through all three, plus the realistic salary numbers a fresh graduate or first jobber in Singapore is actually working with in 2026.

You don't need a huge salary to make this work. You need a budget that's honest about what a first paycheck can carry, and a checklist that stops you signing something you'll regret in month two.

## What a First Job in Singapore Actually Pays

Before you can budget for rent, you need a realistic number for income. Singapore's median gross monthly salary for fresh graduates from the autonomous universities was around S$4,500 in the most recent data, though the range is wide. Polytechnic graduates typically start closer to S$3,000, private education graduates around S$3,500, and degree holders in fields like computing or law can start at S$6,000 or more. Entry-level or service roles without a degree often begin between S$2,000 and S$3,000.

| Background | Typical starting gross monthly salary |
|---|---|
| Autonomous university graduates (median) | around S$4,500 |
| Computing, law and similar high-demand degrees | S$6,000 or more |
| Private education graduates | around S$3,500 |
| Polytechnic graduates | around S$3,000 |
| Entry-level or service roles without a degree | S$2,000 to S$3,000 |

The honest range for a 22 year old's first paycheck in Singapore is roughly S$2,500 to S$4,500 gross, before CPF. That's the number to budget against, not the headline S$4,500 median, unless your degree and industry put you at or above it.

## The Budget Rule of Thumb: 30% of Net Income

The most commonly cited rule for rent affordability in Singapore is to keep rent at around 30% of your **net** monthly income, after CPF contributions. CPF takes roughly 20% off your gross pay, so a S$3,500 gross salary is closer to S$2,800 net, which puts a 30% rent budget at around S$840 a month.

Run your own numbers the same way:

1. **Take your gross monthly salary.**
2. **Subtract roughly 20% for CPF** to get a rough net figure.
3. **Multiply that net figure by 0.30.** That's your rent ceiling, not your target.
4. **Leave room to go lower.** Many people renting their first room comfortably sit at 25% rather than the full 30%, especially in year one when income is still new and irregular costs (a laptop, work clothes, transport top-ups) show up more than expected.

Room prices in Singapore vary by type. HDB common rooms generally run S$800 to S$1,300 and HDB master rooms S$1,300 to S$2,000. Condo rooms sit higher, roughly S$1,000 to S$1,600 for a common room. Co-living rooms span the widest range, since the price usually bundles in furniture, utilities, and wifi rather than billing them separately, which matters a lot when you're comparing a "cheaper" unfurnished room against a furnished one and forgetting to add back the setup cost.

| Room type | Typical monthly rent | Note |
|---|---|---|
| HDB common room | S$800 to S$1,300 | Usually unfurnished, utilities billed separately |
| HDB master room | S$1,300 to S$2,000 | Own bathroom, higher entry point |
| Condo common room | S$1,000 to S$1,600 | Building facilities included, room contents vary |
| Co-living room | Widest range of the four | Price usually bundles furniture, utilities and wifi, so compare against an unfurnished room only after adding back the setup cost |

On a S$2,800 to S$3,500 net income, that generally points a first-time renter toward a shared HDB common room, a co-living room, or a room-share arrangement rather than a full condo unit or a master room on their own. That's not a downgrade, it's just where the math lands for most 22 year olds, and it's exactly the segment co-living was built for. ## Budget Beyond Rent

Rent is the number everyone fixates on, but it's not the whole monthly cost. Add these on top of whatever your room costs:

- **Utilities**, S$100 to S$200 if shared, more on your own meter
- **Phone plan**, S$8 to S$12 for a basic SIM
- **Transport**, roughly S$100 to S$130 a month on public transport
- **Food and groceries**, S$300 to S$450 depending on how much you cook
- **A small buffer**, at least S$100 to S$150 for the month-one surprises

| Cost on top of rent | Monthly range |
|---|---|

## The Signing Checklist: 8 Things to Check Before You Commit

Run through this before you hand over a deposit, not after.

1. **What's actually included in the rent.** Utilities, wifi, and furniture should be spelled out, not assumed. A "cheap" room with everything billed separately can end up costing more than a slightly pricier room with it bundled in.
2. **The deposit and what gets it back.** Ask exactly what condition the room needs to be in at move-out, and get the deposit terms in writing, not verbally.
3. **The notice period.** Most tenancy agreements need one to two months' notice before you can move out. Know this number before you sign, because it determines how much flexibility you have if the job or the living situation changes.
4. **The lease length against your own timeline.** A 12-month lease when you're three months into a probation period is a real commitment. Check whether a shorter or more flexible term is available if you're not certain you'll stay in the same job, or same part of Singapore, a year from now.
5. **Who else lives there and how the space is actually run.** For shared housing, ask about the other housemates, house rules, and who handles maintenance issues. In co-living setups this is usually managed for you; in a private room-share it often isn't.
6. **The room itself, not just the listing photos.** View it in person, or ask for a recent video walkthrough, before committing. Check water pressure, aircon condition, and whether the room gets natural light, none of which show up well in a wide-angle listing photo.
7. **Agent fees, if there's an agent involved.** Know upfront whether a fee applies and who pays it, tenant or landlord, since this varies by listing.
8. **How the agreement handles early termination.** Life changes. A clause that lets you sublet, find a replacement tenant, or exit with a defined penalty is worth more than it looks at signing time.

If a listing or landlord won't give you a straight answer on any of these eight, treat that as information, not just an inconvenience.

## Timing It Around a New Job

The order matters more than people expect. Two timing mistakes come up again and again with first jobbers.

**Signing a lease before an offer is confirmed.** Wait until you have a signed offer letter, not just a verbal one, before committing to a room. Start dates slip, offers occasionally fall through, and a lease is a much harder thing to unwind than a room search.

**Moving during your first week of work.** Probation weeks are already a lot to absorb. Where possible, move in the week before you start, or plan your move-in date for a weekend so you're settled before your first Monday, not scrambling to unpack after a full day at a new desk.

A workable sequence looks like this: confirm the offer, budget against your actual net pay using the 30% rule above, shortlist two or three rooms, run the 8-point checklist on each, then sign with enough runway to move in before your start date rather than after it.
