A PhD stipend in Singapore runs roughly S$2,000 to S$3,800 a month depending on the university, citizenship status, and whether you've cleared your qualifying exam. On that income, a private studio is out of reach for most students, but a common room in an HDB flat, a room in a condo share, or a bed in a co-living space are all realistic. The right choice usually comes down to how close you need to be to campus and how much hassle you're willing to take on setting up a home from scratch.

This isn't a budgeting breakdown with percentages and formulas. It's a straight look at PhD student housing in Singapore: what stipends actually pay, what that money buys in the current rental market, and where PhD students tend to end up living.

## How much do PhD stipends actually pay in Singapore?

At NUS, PhD candidates on the Research Scholarship receive S$3,800 a month if they're Singapore Citizens, S$3,400 for Permanent Residents, and S$3,000 for international students, effective from 1 January 2026. Tuition is covered separately. Scholars can get an extra S$500 a month after passing their qualifying exam, which usually lands sometime in year one or two.

At NTU, the Research Scholarship starts lower: S$2,700 a month for citizens, S$2,200 for PRs, and S$2,000 for international students, rising by up to S$500 after the qualifying exam. NTU's Nanyang President's Graduate Scholarship pays similar starting rates. Some NTU programmes, like the NIE PhD track, pay closer to S$3,000 to S$3,800 depending on funding source.

## What does that stipend actually buy in Singapore's rental market?

As of mid-2026, a common room in an HDB flat runs from roughly S$800 to S$1,300 a month, clustering around S$900 in most non-central towns. A common room in a condo tends to sit closer to S$1,300. Master rooms, the larger bedroom with its own bathroom, run higher again, often above S$2,000 in a condo.

Cheaper HDB common rooms tend to be in Choa Chu Kang, Woodlands, Yishun, Jurong West, and Hougang. Pricier ones sit in more central or mature estates like Queenstown, Bukit Merah, Kallang, and Toa Payoh. That's before utilities, wifi, and any agent fee, which HDB landlords don't always bundle in.

## What about deposits, agent fees, and lease length?

Most HDB common room rentals in Singapore ask for a security deposit, typically one to two months' rent, plus an agent fee if you found the room through a property agent rather than direct from the landlord. Standard leases run six months to a year, and breaking one early usually means forfeiting the deposit unless your tenancy agreement says otherwise.

That upfront cash outlay catches a lot of incoming PhD students off guard, especially international students arriving without a Singapore bank account or a local guarantor yet. Some landlords also want to see proof of income or a scholarship letter before they'll rent to a student at all, so it's worth having your offer letter and stipend confirmation ready before you start viewing rooms.

Co-living operators tend to run this differently. Lease terms are usually shorter and more flexible, deposits are lower or simpler, and because the room comes furnished and the utilities and wifi are already bundled into the rent, there's no separate deposit for a router, no PUB account to open, and no furniture to source in your first week in the country.

## Should you try for on-campus housing first?

Worth applying for, but don't bank on it. NUS gives first-year PhD students special consideration for a spot in University Town housing, but only for their first two semesters, and NUS itself says demand outstrips supply every intake. Not every applicant gets a room, and NUS explicitly advises students to look for off-campus housing in parallel rather than wait on the outcome.

NTU runs its own halls and graduate residences with similar constraints: limited beds, priority tiers, and no guarantee once you're past your first year or two. Treat campus housing as a bonus if it comes through, not the base plan. Have an off-campus option lined up regardless.

## Which neighbourhoods make sense on a PhD budget?

For NUS students, Clementi, Dover, Buona Vista, and Pasir Panjang sit within a short bus or MRT ride of Kent Ridge and tend to have a decent supply of common rooms without the premium of the most central estates. For NTU students, Boon Lay, Pioneer, and the wider Jurong area put you close to campus on the Circle or East-West line without paying central-Singapore rent.

If you're willing to commute a bit further for a meaningfully lower rent, the north and northeast corridors, areas like Yishun, Woodlands, Serangoon, and Hougang, consistently price below the island average for HDB common rooms. Many PhD students end up trading twenty extra minutes on the MRT for a few hundred dollars saved a month, which adds up fast over a four-year candidature.

## What room type actually fits a stipend?

A shared common room in an HDB flat is the cheapest way into decent housing, but you're taking on a flatmate situation you don't control, a landlord relationship with no professional management behind it, and often a lease that assumes a longer commitment than a first PhD year really needs. A private room in a co-living setup costs more than the cheapest HDB common rooms but less than a condo master room, and it comes with a shorter, more flexible lease, a managed community instead of a random flat-share, and none of the furniture-buying and utility-account-opening that a fresh HDB tenancy involves.

It's a genuine tradeoff, and it's worth weighing before you sign a year-long lease you can't easily exit if your research takes you overseas for a semester or your funding situation changes.

## What do most PhD students actually end up doing?

In practice, a lot of first-year PhD students take whatever campus housing they're offered, since it's cheap and it means one less thing to sort out while you're also settling into a lab, a supervisor, and a new degree structure. Once that first-year priority window ends, most move off campus, and that's usually when the tradeoffs above start to matter.

International students without an existing Singapore network tend to lean toward co-living or a room found through an agent, since both come with someone else handling the logistics. Local students and PRs, who usually have family, friends, or at least a bank account and a guarantor already in place, are more likely to go straight for an HDB common room and accept the extra legwork for the lower rent.

Neither path is the "correct" one. It depends on how much your time is worth in your first few months, versus how much you want to shave off the monthly number.

## A realistic starting point

Don't try to solve the whole four years of your candidature on day one. Apply for on-campus housing if you're eligible, treat it as a maybe, and line up an off-campus option at the same time so you're not scrambling in your first week. Look at neighbourhoods near your campus first, then widen the search if the rent doesn't fit your stipend.
