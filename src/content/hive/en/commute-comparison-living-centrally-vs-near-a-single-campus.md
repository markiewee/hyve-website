Living centrally, near a well-connected MRT interchange, usually beats living next to a single campus, unless that campus is genuinely the only place you need to reach on a regular basis. A room right by campus wins the one commute it's built for and nothing else. A central room adds a few extra minutes to that same campus run, but keeps every other destination reachable too: an internship in the CBD, a part-time job, a second campus, friends scattered across the island. The right call comes down to how many places you actually need to get to each week, not how short you can make a single trip.

## What Does "Living Centrally" Actually Mean?

It doesn't mean living in the most expensive postcode. It means living near a station where more than one MRT line meets, an interchange, so you have more than one direct route out without having to double back through a transfer point later.

Singapore has a handful of these that matter for exactly this reason. Dhoby Ghaut connects the North South, North East and Circle Lines. City Hall connects the North South and East West Lines. Jurong East connects the North South and East West Lines on the west side of the island. Living near any of these, or near a station one or two stops off one, means most of the island is reachable on a single line or a single clean change, not a long detour.

Campus-adjacent housing is the opposite bet. It's usually built around one line, sometimes served only by a bus, and optimised for one trip: home to lecture hall. That trip will be short. Almost everything else won't be.

## The Case for Living Next to One Campus

If your campus really is the only fixed point in your week, living next to it is hard to beat. You roll out of bed later, you're not budgeting transfer time into every class, and bad weather or a late night before an exam costs you far less. For a student with no internship, no part-time job across town, and a social circle mostly on or near campus, this is a genuinely rational choice, not a lazy one.

It also tends to be cheaper to search for, since campus-adjacent housing markets are smaller and more predictable. You know roughly what's available and roughly what it costs, without weighing an entire island's worth of options.

## Where Campus-Adjacent Housing Strands You

The catch shows up the moment your week stops being just campus. A CBD internship, common in the second half of most degrees, can turn a five-minute walk to lecture into an hour-plus trip to Raffles Place or Tanjong Pagar, because campus-adjacent stations are rarely built for a run into town. A part-time job in a different part of the island has the same problem. So does a friend group that doesn't all live near your one campus, or a partner who studies or works somewhere else entirely.

The pattern is the same in every case: a location tuned to minimise one specific trip has no slack for the trips you didn't plan around. And student weeks rarely stay as simple as they look in first year.

## Living Centrally vs Living Next to One Campus

| | Living Centrally (near an interchange) | Living Next to One Campus |
|---|---|---|
| Commute to your main campus | A few minutes longer than the campus-adjacent option | Shortest possible, often walkable |
| Commute to everywhere else | Reasonable, usually one line or one clean change | Often long, sometimes requiring a bus plus a transfer |
| Resilience to a changed schedule | High, an internship or new campus rarely breaks the location | Low, one new fixed point can undo the whole logic |
| Housing search | Wider pool, more listings to compare | Smaller, more predictable pool near one campus |
| Best for | Anyone with more than one regular destination in their week | Anyone whose week genuinely has one destination, full stop |

## The Real Math: One Trip a Day vs a Scattered Week

The comparison isn't really "short commute vs long commute." It's "one commute vs several." A campus-adjacent room optimises a single number: minutes to your main campus. A central room accepts a slightly worse version of that one number in exchange for a decent version of every other number in your week.

Do the arithmetic honestly before choosing. Count the destinations you'll actually need to reach on a normal week, not just campus: a job, an internship, a gym, a second campus, a friend's place, the airport for term breaks. If that list has one entry, optimise for that entry and live next to campus. If it has three or four, optimising for one of them at the expense of the rest usually costs more time overall than the few minutes you saved on the trip you optimised.

## When Campus-Adjacent Still Wins, Even With a Busy Week

There's one case where campus-adjacent housing keeps winning even against a busy schedule: when the campus itself sits on or near a major interchange, so proximity to campus and proximity to everything else aren't actually in tension. That's the exception, not the rule, and it's worth checking your specific campus's station against the interchange list above before assuming you have to choose.

The other exception is genuinely short stints, an exchange semester or a summer programme, where the extra effort of being central for three months of scattered errands isn't worth trading against a longer commute for something you're only there to attend.

## Two Situations Where This Gets More Complicated

Everything above assumes one campus and a normal week around it. Two situations change the math on their own terms and deserve their own treatment rather than a few extra paragraphs here.

If you're taking modules across more than one campus in the same semester, a common setup for students cross-registering between universities, the calculation isn't campus vs everywhere else, it's campus A vs campus B vs everywhere else. ## How to Actually Decide

Work through this before signing anything:

- List every place you need to reach on a normal week, not just campus
- If that list has one entry, weight the decision toward campus-adjacent
- If it has three or more, weight it toward a well-connected interchange area
- Check whether your specific campus sits near a major interchange already, which can remove the tradeoff entirely
- Run the actual commute for every entry on your list, not just campus, using Google Maps or Citymapper at a weekday peak departure
- Re-run the list again before your final year or an internship season, since the list that was true in year one rarely stays true for the whole degree

## Frequently Asked Questions

**Is it better to live centrally or near campus in Singapore?**
It depends on how many places you need to reach regularly. If campus is genuinely your only fixed destination, living next to it wins. If your week includes an internship, a job, a second campus or a scattered friend group, a central location near an MRT interchange usually costs only a few extra minutes to campus while keeping everything else reachable.

**What counts as "central" for a student in Singapore?**
Generally a location near an MRT interchange, a station where two or more lines meet, such as Dhoby Ghaut, City Hall or Jurong East. Living near one of these, or a stop or two off one, means most of the island is reachable on one line or one clean transfer.

**Does living next to campus save that much time compared to living centrally?**
Usually a few minutes on the one trip it's built for. The real cost shows up on every other trip, where a campus-adjacent location often has no direct route and a central one usually does.

**What if I don't know yet whether I'll need to travel beyond campus?**
Assume you will. Internships, part-time jobs and cross-registration are common enough in most Singapore degrees that betting on a campus-only week for four years is usually the riskier assumption, not the safer one.
