The cost of living in Singapore for Indian nationals in 2026 typically runs S$1,800 to S$3,200 a month for a single professional renting a room, before rent for a whole unit is added. That covers food, transport, phone and wifi, an Indian-grocery habit, and a modest remittance home. Add rent for a room in a shared home, usually S$800 to S$1,800 depending on room type and location, and a realistic all-in monthly number lands between S$2,600 and S$4,500. Someone renting a whole unit alone should budget noticeably higher, since rent for a full apartment starts well above what a room costs.

Those numbers move a lot depending on three things specific to an Indian national's move: which work pass you're on, how much of your Indian-food habit you keep up locally, and how much you're sending home each month. This piece breaks all three down, with a full budget at the end.

## What does it cost to live in Singapore as an Indian national in 2026?

Singapore's general cost of living for a single person, excluding rent, runs roughly S$1,500 to S$1,900 a month on Numbeo's 2026 data, covering groceries, transport, phone, and everyday spending. That figure isn't built around any one nationality; it's a general baseline.

For an Indian national specifically, the number shifts two ways. Indian staples, spices, dal, atta, and ghee push the food line up, since imported ingredients cost more here than in India. Remittances home are also a real recurring cost a generic cost-of-living figure skips entirely.

Indian nationals are one of the largest foreign professional communities in Singapore, concentrated in IT, banking, fintech, and engineering roles on Employment Pass and S Pass. That community size is why Little India and Serangoon have built up dense Indian grocery, food, and religious infrastructure that keeps day-to-day costs manageable.

## Rent: the single biggest line item

Rent dominates the monthly number more than any other cost. A room in a shared home in Singapore typically runs S$800 to S$1,800 a month depending on room type, with all-inclusive co-living rooms bundling utilities and wifi into that figure. A whole studio or one-bedroom unit runs considerably more, and almost never includes utilities in the headline rent.

The full breakdown of what rent actually costs across room types, whole units, and what's included versus billed separately is covered in [cost of renting in Singapore 2026](https://www.lazybee.sg/hive/cost-of-renting-singapore-2026-budget). That piece isn't India-specific, but the rent math is the same regardless of where you're moving from. This article focuses on what changes on top of that baseline for an Indian national's move.

## Indian groceries and food costs in Singapore

Cooking with Indian staples in Singapore costs more than it does in India, generally two to three times more for imported items like specific dals, spices, and ghee brands. A monthly Indian-pantry grocery habit sourced from Mustafa Centre, Tekka Centre, or Little India grocers typically runs S$200 to S$350 a month for a single person cooking regularly at home.

Little India and Serangoon are the two neighbourhoods where this cost is easiest to manage, since both have a dense cluster of Indian grocers, wet markets, and restaurants competing on price. Sourcing basics there costs noticeably less than buying the same items at a general supermarket.

Eating out doesn't have to be expensive either. A hawker meal, including South Indian and North Indian stalls in most hawker centres, runs S$5 to S$10. Mixing hawker meals with home cooking and the occasional restaurant meal, a realistic monthly food budget is S$400 to S$700 for someone cooking often, or S$700 to S$1,000 for someone eating out more.

## Work passes: what they mean for the budget, not just the paperwork

Most Indian nationals moving to Singapore for work arrive on an Employment Pass or S Pass, and the pass type affects the budget indirectly through the minimum salary tied to it. As of this year, the Employment Pass requires a minimum qualifying monthly salary of roughly S$5,600 for general sectors, and the S Pass requires roughly S$3,300, both rising with age and experience.

These are minimums set by the Ministry of Manpower, not typical offer levels, so most incoming professionals land above the floor. Someone earning well above the EP minimum has far more budget room than someone closer to the S Pass minimum.

## Sending money back to India: remittance costs

For most Indian nationals living in Singapore, sending money home is a genuine recurring cost that a generic cost-of-living guide skips entirely. The good news is that the SGD to INR corridor is one of the cheapest remittance routes in the world, since so much volume runs through it.

Digital-first providers like Wise and Instarem consistently offer rates close to the mid-market exchange rate, with fees running a fraction of what traditional bank wire transfers charge. Bank transfers from a Singapore bank directly to an Indian account are simple but usually carry a wider margin on the exchange rate than a dedicated remittance app.

Budget the remittance itself as whatever amount you choose to send, but budget the fee and exchange-rate spread separately: a well-chosen provider keeps that cost under 1% of the transfer, while a bank wire or a poorly chosen provider can quietly cost several times that.

## Flights and staying connected to family

Round-trip flights between Singapore and major Indian cities are frequent and, outside peak season, reasonably priced. Off-peak round trips from Chennai or Bangalore typically run S$150 to S$250, with Mumbai somewhat higher. Peak season around Diwali and Christmas pushes fares up sharply, so book six to eight weeks ahead for those dates.

Budgeting one or two trips home a year, plus an occasional emergency fare, is a realistic line item most Singapore cost-of-living guides skip entirely.

## Where Indian professionals and students tend to live

Little India and the areas around Serangoon are the obvious first answer: groceries, temples, restaurants, and a built-in community make the adjustment easier. But for many working professionals they're not the most practical base once commute to the CBD or a business park is factored in.

Co-living rooms across the island put you within reach of Indian grocers and food without requiring you to live in Little India itself, since MRT access makes a 15 to 20 minute trip to Tekka Centre or Mustafa Centre routine. ## A realistic monthly budget for an Indian national in Singapore

| Item | Typical monthly cost |
|---|---|
| Room rent (shared home, often all-inclusive) | S$800 to S$1,800 |
| Food, including Indian groceries | S$400 to S$700 |
| Transport (public transport, daily commute) | S$100 to S$150 |
| Phone and wifi (where not bundled into rent) | S$30 to S$60 |
| Remittance to India (fee and spread only, not the transfer amount itself) | S$10 to S$40 |
| Flights home (averaged monthly, 1 to 2 trips a year) | S$50 to S$100 |
| **Total, room + living costs** | **S$1,400 to S$2,850** |

This is a working professional's baseline, not a hard ceiling. Someone renting a whole unit alone instead of a room should add roughly S$1,000 to S$2,500 more to the rent line, since a full apartment costs considerably more than a shared room and rarely includes utilities.

## Myth: Indian groceries make Singapore unaffordable

This gets said a lot, and it's overstated. Yes, specific imported Indian ingredients cost two to three times what they cost in India. But that's one line in a monthly budget, not the whole budget, and it's a smaller line than most people assume before they actually start shopping in Little India or Serangoon.

The bigger cost driver by far is rent, the same as it is for every nationality moving to Singapore. Anyone comparing "cost of living in Singapore" headline figures against India should be comparing rent-adjusted numbers, not grocery receipts, since rent is where the real gap sits.

## Frequently asked questions

**What is a realistic monthly budget for an Indian national living in Singapore in 2026?**
For a single professional renting a room, roughly S$2,600 to S$4,500 a month all-in, covering rent, food including Indian groceries, transport, phone, remittance costs, and an averaged flight budget. Renting a whole unit alone pushes that meaningfully higher.

**Are Indian groceries expensive in Singapore?**
Imported Indian staples like specific spices, dals, and ghee typically cost two to three times what they cost in India. A monthly Indian-pantry grocery budget for one person, sourced from Little India or Serangoon, typically runs S$200 to S$350.

**What's the cheapest way to send money from Singapore to India?**
Digital-first remittance apps like Wise and Instarem generally beat traditional bank wire transfers on both fee and exchange-rate spread for the SGD-INR corridor. Compare live quotes before choosing, since rates shift.

**Do I need an Employment Pass or S Pass to rent in Singapore?**
You need a valid pass to rent legally as a foreigner, and most landlords and co-living operators will ask to see it as part of document checks. The specifics by pass type are covered in [can foreigners rent in Singapore](https://www.lazybee.sg/hive/can-foreigners-rent-in-singapore).

**Is it cheaper to live in Little India as an Indian national in Singapore?**
Groceries and food are generally cheaper there due to competition among Indian grocers, but rent in Little India itself isn't necessarily lower than other central areas. Many Indian professionals live elsewhere on the island and travel to Little India or Serangoon for groceries rather than living there specifically for that reason.
