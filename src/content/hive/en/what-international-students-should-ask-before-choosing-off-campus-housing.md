Before you sign anything, ask about lease length against your Student Pass validity, what's actually included in the rent, the deposit and refund process, whether guests and subletting are allowed, the real commute, whether the room is furnished, what happens if you need to break the lease, and who else lives in the building. None of these are trick questions. They're the ones that separate a listing that looks fine in photos from a place that actually works for your course.

The reason this matters more for international students than for locals is simple: you usually can't just drive over and check. You're deciding from another country, often in a rush before a semester starts, without a local friend to sense-check what you're being told. A landlord's answer to each question below tells you more than the answer itself. It tells you how they run the place.

| Ask | A good answer sounds like | A warning sign sounds like |
|---|---|---|
| Does the lease length match my Student Pass? | They raise it before you do, and adjust the end date on paper | "It'll be fine", with nothing changed in the agreement |
| What is actually included in the rent? | A specific list: wifi, electricity, water, common-area cleaning, any usage cap, and the overage charge | "It's mostly covered." The word "mostly" is usually covering a gap that shows on your first bill |
| What is the deposit, and how does the refund work? | An amount, the conditions for a full refund, a timeline in writing, and a photo record at move-in | "We'll assess it when the time comes" |
| Are guests and subletting allowed? | A clear policy: how many nights, whether overnight guests need notice, whether subletting is possible with approval | "Just don't make it a problem" |
| What is the real commute? | Which bus or train, how many transfers, and what the trip looks like outside best-case timing | A single headline number built for a listing |
| Is the room furnished, and with what? | A specific list, plus current photos of your actual room | Photos of "a similar unit that looks the same" |
| What if I need to break the lease early? | A notice period and a stated fee or forfeited deposit portion, in the agreement | "We'll work something out" |
| Who else lives in the building? | A loose but real description of the current house and its mix | No idea who else lives there |

## Does the Lease Length Actually Match Your Student Pass?

Start here, since it's the question that can quietly unravel everything else. Your Student Pass is tied to your course duration, and if your lease runs longer or shorter than that window, you're exposed either way. A lease that outlasts your pass leaves you paying rent on a room you may not legally be able to occupy. A lease shorter than your course means finding new housing mid-term.

Ask the landlord directly whether they're comfortable aligning the lease end date to your programme, not just to a standard 12-month cycle. A landlord who works with international students regularly will ask you this first, before you have to raise it.

The red flag isn't a fixed 12-month term itself, plenty of good operators only offer that. The red flag is a landlord who brushes off the question or says "it'll be fine" without adjusting anything on paper. If your pass expires mid-lease and nothing in the agreement acknowledges that, you're the one holding the risk, not them.

## What's Actually Included in the Rent?

"All inclusive" gets used loosely, and it means something different at every building. Ask for the specific list: wifi, electricity, water, common area cleaning, and whether any of those carry a usage cap, plus what happens if you go over it. A fair use limit is normal. An unexplained penalty charge is not.

This matters more for students than working tenants because of budget predictability. You're likely managing a fixed allowance from home or a stipend, and a rent figure that turns out to be a floor rather than a ceiling can wreck a semester's budget fast. A landlord who can list exactly what's covered, without checking a spreadsheet, runs a tight operation. One who says "don't worry, it's mostly covered" is worth pushing harder before you sign.

Listen for the word "mostly." It's usually covering a gap that only shows up on your first bill.

## What's the Deposit, and What's the Actual Refund Process?

Ask three things: how much the deposit is, what conditions get it back in full, and how long the refund actually takes after you move out. The amount matters, but the process matters more. A deposit that's technically refundable but takes three months and five follow-up messages to land isn't much better than one you never get back.

Ask specifically what counts as normal wear and tear versus something that gets deducted. A landlord who can describe this clearly, ideally with an inspection or photo record at move-in, won't be inventing damage charges at move-out. One who gets vague or says "we'll assess it when the time comes" is telling you the process isn't documented, which means it isn't predictable.

Get the refund timeline in writing if you can. A month's difference in when that money lands is real when you're also paying a deposit on your next place, in another currency.

## Are Guests and Subletting Actually Allowed?

This one trips up more students than any other, usually out of assumption rather than dishonesty. You might plan to have a partner stay over for a week, or sublet your room during a summer trip home. Ask upfront what the actual policy is, not what you assume is normal.

Ask before signing rather than after moving in, because subletting without permission is a straightforward breach of most tenancy and licence agreements in Singapore, and it puts your own standing at risk, not just the arrangement itself. A landlord who lays out a clear guest policy, how many nights, whether overnight guests need notice, whether subletting is possible with approval, has thought about this before. One who says "just don't make it a problem" hasn't, and that vagueness becomes your problem the day it actually comes up.

If you already know you'll be away for a stretch of the semester, raise it at the viewing. It's a much easier conversation before there's a lease to breach.

## What's the Real Commute, Not the Advertised One?

Listings measure distance in straight lines. You'll be measuring it in transfers, walk time, and how it feels doing that trip at 8am with a bag on your back. "15 minutes to campus" and "15 minutes if you catch the bus at exactly the right moment, otherwise 35" are both technically true, and only one is useful.

Ask the landlord to walk you through the actual route: which bus or train, how many transfers, what the trip looks like outside best-case timing. Better still, check the trip yourself on a map with real transit timing rather than trusting the marketing distance. A landlord who can describe the commute in specific, unglamorous detail is being straight with you. One who only offers a headline number is giving you the version built for a listing photo, not your actual Tuesday morning.

Check it against your real timetable too, not just campus location generally, since a 15-minute walk to one faculty building can be a very different trip to a lecture hall on the other side of a large campus.

## Is the Room Actually Furnished, and With What?

"Furnished" can mean a bed frame and nothing else, or a bed, mattress, wardrobe, desk, and chair ready to use the day you land. That gap is the difference between unpacking a suitcase and furnishing a room from scratch in a country you've just arrived in, before you've worked out where to buy anything.

Ask for a specific list of what's in the room, and for photos of the actual room you'd be taking, not a similar unit elsewhere in the building. Rooms in the same building can differ more than you'd expect. A landlord who sends current photos without hesitation has nothing to hide. One who stalls, or offers photos from a different unit "that looks the same," is a signal to slow down and ask why.

## What Happens If You Need to Break the Lease Early?

Study plans change. Exchange semesters end early, courses get deferred, family situations pull you home. Ask what an early exit costs and how much notice you need, before you're in a position where you actually need to use that clause.

A well-run listing has a clear early termination clause: a notice period, and either a fee or a forfeited portion of deposit, spelled out in the agreement rather than left to a conversation after the fact. Listen for specificity. "We'll work something out" sounds friendly at a viewing and becomes a real problem the day you need to leave and there's nothing on paper to point to.

## Are There Other Students or International Residents in the Building?

This isn't just about social fit, though that matters when you're moving somewhere new without an existing circle. It's also a signal about how the building runs. A landlord experienced with international and student tenants has usually already solved problems you haven't thought to ask about: how mail works if you're not always in, how deposits get returned across currencies, how a mid-lease Student Pass renewal gets handled.

Ask who else lives there, roughly what mix of local and international, working and studying. You're listening for whether the landlord actually knows their own building or is guessing. One who can describe the current house, even loosely, has been paying attention. One with no idea who else lives there is either new to this or not particularly involved, and you'll be the one filling that gap once you move in.

## Ask the Questions Before You Ask for the Room

None of these questions are unreasonable, and a landlord who's run this before will answer all eight without flinching. The ones worth walking away from are the ones who get vague, defensive, or rush you toward a signature. Slow answers you can work with. Evasive ones you can't.

## FAQ

**Should my lease length match my Student Pass exactly?**
Ideally yes, or close enough that neither side is left exposed. A landlord experienced with student tenants should be able to align the lease end date to your programme rather than forcing a standard 12-month term regardless of your actual timeline.

**What's a reasonable notice period for breaking a lease early?**
It varies by operator, but the number matters less than whether it's written down. A vague promise to "work something out" isn't a substitute for a clause you can point to if your plans change.

**Is it normal for co-living rent to have a utility usage cap?**
Yes, a fair use cap is standard and reasonable. What matters is whether it's disclosed upfront, set at a level an ordinary tenant won't easily exceed, and what the overage charge is if you do.

**Should I ask for photos of the exact room I'd be renting?**
Always. Rooms in the same building can differ, and a photo of "a similar unit" isn't the same as your actual room. A landlord with nothing to hide sends current photos without hesitation.
