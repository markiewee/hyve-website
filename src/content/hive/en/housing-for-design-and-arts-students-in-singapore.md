Design and arts students need something most student housing guides don't account for: a room that can absorb irregular studio hours, hold a portfolio piece without taking over the bed, and stay livable through exhibition week. That's the real brief, whether you're at LASALLE College of the Arts, NAFA, or a design diploma at a polytechnic. Regular student housing advice assumes a 9-to-5 timetable and a laptop bag. Studio-based courses don't work that way, and your room needs to match the course, not a generic student profile.

This piece covers the category as a whole, what studio-based learning actually demands from where you live, and how to judge a room against it. For the specific commute numbers and campus details of LASALLE or NAFA, we've written those up separately, linked below.

## Which Singapore institutions actually run studio-based design and arts courses?

**LASALLE College of the Arts** runs its programmes across two campuses in the Bras Basah/Bugis arts precinct, McNally Campus and Winstedt Campus. Design is split across the School of Spatial & Product Design, the School of Fashion, and the School of Design Communication, alongside a Fine Arts pathway running from diploma through to MA. In March 2021, LASALLE formed an alliance with NAFA to create the University of the Arts Singapore, though each school kept its own campus.

**NAFA (Nanyang Academy of Fine Arts)** occupies four campuses on and around Bencoolen Street, covering Design and Media, Fine Art, 3D Design, Fashion Studies, Arts Management, Dance, Theatre, and Music. It's the other half of the University of the Arts Singapore alliance.

**The polytechnics** run design diplomas too, and they're a bigger slice of Singapore's design student population than people assume. Temasek Polytechnic has a dedicated School of Design at its Tampines campus. Nanyang Polytechnic runs design through its School of Design & Media, with diploma pathways including Animation, Games & Visual Effects, Architecture, Communication & Motion Design, and Experience Design. Other polytechnics run their own design and media tracks too.

## Why studio-based learning changes what you need from a room

A design or arts diploma isn't structured like a business or engineering course. Studio modules run on project time, not class time. You might have a light Tuesday and then a stretch of late nights before a submission, because the work genuinely takes that long.

Polytechnic and college studios keep extended hours precisely because of this. Temasek Polytechnic, for example, keeps its campus open from 6:30am to 10:30pm on weekdays and until 6pm on Saturdays, well past a standard school day. The institutions themselves are built around students working outside normal hours, and your housing should be too.

That means your room needs to handle you coming home late without waking a household, with a desk and outlet setup that works at midnight the same as midday. A room that's fine for a 6pm-to-10pm study routine can fall apart once your actual schedule is closer to 9am to 1am on a deadline week.

## The real portfolio problem: space and quiet

Portfolio-based courses generate physical output. Fine Art and 3D Design students end up with canvases, models, or finished pieces that need somewhere to sit that isn't the middle of the room. Fashion and product design students often have a sewing machine, fabric, or prototypes eating into floor space. Even primarily digital courses, like communication design or motion design, come with the practical need to leave a laptop rendering or exporting overnight without it competing for desk space the next morning.

| Course type | Physical output that needs room | What that means for the space |
|---|---|---|
| Fine Art, 3D Design | Canvases, models, finished pieces | Somewhere they can sit that is not the middle of the room |
| Fashion, product design | A sewing machine, fabric, prototypes | Real floor space, not just a desk |
| Communication design, motion design | Mostly digital | A laptop left rendering or exporting overnight, without it competing for desk space the next morning |

"Space" here isn't abstract square footage. It's whether the room has a corner, a shelf, or a bit of floor that's usable for work in progress, not just a bed and a nightstand. Ask this directly on any viewing, since it varies room to room even within the same property.

"Quiet" matters just as much and gets less attention. Studio work often means headphones-off concentration for hours, or the opposite, playing something on repeat while working through a piece. A thin wall next to a housemate on a different schedule is a real friction point a floor plan won't show you.

## What exhibition and critique season means for your living situation

Studio courses run on crit and exhibition cycles, not just exam weeks. A critique session where you present unfinished or finished work to tutors and classmates can run long and land at an unpredictable time. Graduation shows and end-of-term exhibitions mean weeks of installation, rehearsal, or final assembly work that doesn't happen on a 9-to-5 clock.

Two things matter here that a generic student housing checklist won't flag. First, a safe, walkable route home from your nearest MRT station late at night, since crit and install nights routinely run past a normal evening. Second, a household that won't be disrupted every time your schedule doesn't match theirs, which is a question about the specific room and housemates, not the property as a whole.

## What to look for in a room as a design or arts student

- Floor or shelf space for work in progress, checked in person, not assumed from a listing photo
- A desk setup and power outlets that work for late-night sessions, not just daytime study
- Realistic noise levels, both what you'll hear from neighbours and what they'll tolerate from you
- A walkable, well-lit route from the nearest MRT for the nights you're coming home late
- Reliable wifi if you're uploading large design files, video, or renders on a deadline
- All-in rent (utilities, wifi, common area cleaning) so a busy term doesn't come with separate bills to chase
- Furnished, move-in-ready rooms, so you're not sourcing and reselling furniture mid-course
- A read on the current household's rhythm before you commit, since that shapes daily life more than the room itself

| Check | Why it matters | How to verify |
|---|---|---|
| Floor or shelf space for work in progress | It varies room to room even inside the same property | In person on the viewing, never from a listing photo |
| Desk and power outlets that work at midnight | A setup fine for 6pm to 10pm falls apart on a 9am to 1am deadline week | Look at where the outlets actually are |
| Realistic noise levels, both directions | A thin wall next to a housemate on a different schedule is a real friction point | Ask about the current household's rhythm |
| A walkable, well-lit route from the MRT | Crit and install nights routinely run past a normal evening | Walk it at the hour you would actually be coming home |
| Reliable wifi | Uploading large design files, video or renders on a deadline | Ask, and test on the viewing |
| All-in rent | A busy term should not come with separate bills to chase | Confirm utilities, wifi and common-area cleaning are bundled |
| Furnished and move-in ready | You are not sourcing and reselling furniture mid-course | Confirm what stays in the room |

## Where co-living fits into this

Shared co-living houses solve the space and schedule problem differently from a hall of residence or a solo studio rental. You get a private, lockable room plus common kitchen and living space, which works well for the informal side of art school: a group crit over coffee in the kitchen, a housemate's honest opinion on a piece before you present it. The tradeoff is the same as any shared household, so it's worth asking about the current housemates before you sign anything.

## Frequently asked questions

**Do design and arts students need different housing from other students?**
The core need, private room, decent wifi, a reasonable commute, is the same. What's different is studio hours running later and less predictably, the need for physical space to store or work on portfolio pieces, and a household that can handle a schedule that doesn't match a typical 9-to-5 student routine.

**Is LASALLE the same as NAFA?**
No. Both merged administratively in March 2021 to form the University of the Arts Singapore, but each kept its own campus, programmes, and identity. LASALLE is based across McNally and Winstedt campuses in Bras Basah/Bugis. NAFA is based on Bencoolen Street, a short walk away in the same precinct.

**Do polytechnic design diplomas have the same late-studio culture as LASALLE or NAFA?**
Largely yes, though it varies by course and school. Extended campus hours at institutions like Temasek Polytechnic reflect the same reality: studio and project work runs past a standard school day, and housing that only works for a 9-to-5 routine won't fit well.

**Is co-living a good fit for a design or arts student with bulky supplies?**
It depends on the specific room and how much storage it offers, which varies within the same house. Check floor space and storage on a viewing rather than assuming any room will fit a full set of studio materials.
