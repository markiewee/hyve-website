The cost of living in Singapore for a Sri Lankan professional or student in 2026 typically runs S$1,450 to S$1,900 a month for everyday costs, before rent. Add a room in a shared home, usually S$800 to S$1,800 depending on room type and location, and a realistic all-in monthly budget lands between S$2,250 and S$3,700. That covers food, transport, phone and wifi, and remittance costs, but not the rent for a whole unit, which runs considerably higher.

This piece is written for Sri Lankan nationals renting in the open market: professionals on an Employment Pass or S Pass, and students. Singapore has a long-established Sri Lankan community, both Sinhalese and Tamil, going back generations, so the community infrastructure for groceries, food, and worship is genuinely well built out. That helps keep day-to-day costs manageable once you know where to look.

## What does it cost to live in Singapore as a Sri Lankan national in 2026?

Singapore's general cost of living for a single person, excluding rent, runs roughly S$1,450 to S$1,900 a month, covering groceries, transport, phone, and everyday spending. Numbeo and Wise both put the baseline single-person figure, excluding rent, at roughly S$1,470 to S$1,510 a month as of mid-2026, and that's a general baseline, not built around any one nationality.

For a Sri Lankan national specifically, two things move the number. Ceylon-style groceries and specific spices cost more here than at home, though far less than most newcomers expect once they find the right shops. Sending money back to Sri Lanka is also a real recurring cost that a generic cost-of-living figure leaves out entirely.

Sri Lankan nationals in Singapore span professionals in finance, tech, and shipping, students at the local universities, and a smaller Work Permit segment. The Ceylon Sports Club at 101 Balestier Road, running since 1928, remains a genuine community anchor, alongside the food and grocery presence in the wider Little India and Race Course Road area.

## Rent: the single biggest line item

Rent dominates the monthly number more than anything else. A room in a shared home typically runs S$800 to S$1,800 a month depending on room type, with all-inclusive co-living rooms bundling utilities and wifi into that one figure. A whole studio or one-bedroom unit costs considerably more and almost never includes utilities in the headline rent.

## Sri Lankan food and groceries: what it actually costs

Cooking with Sri Lankan staples in Singapore, coconut milk, curry leaves, Ceylon spices, roasted curry powder, costs more than sourcing the same ingredients at home, but it's manageable once you know where to shop. A monthly grocery habit built around these staples typically runs S$150 to S$300 a month for a single person cooking regularly.

Little India and Race Course Road carry some of the most concentrated South Indian and Sri Lankan food in the city, including the Michelin-starred Sri Lankan restaurant Kotuwa and newer spots like Podi & Poriyal. Smaller Sri Lankan grocery outfits also operate for staples that aren't stocked in a regular supermarket; check current stock and prices directly since a small-format grocery scene shifts store to store.

Eating out doesn't have to be expensive. A hawker meal runs S$5 to S$10, and Little India's food centres carry a strong South Asian hawker selection. Mixing hawker meals with home cooking, a realistic monthly food budget is S$350 to S$600 for someone cooking often, or S$650 to S$950 for someone eating out more.

## Work passes: what they mean for the budget

Most Sri Lankan professionals arrive on an Employment Pass or S Pass, and the pass type shapes the budget indirectly through its minimum qualifying salary. As of 2026, the Employment Pass requires S$5,600 a month for most sectors and S$6,200 for financial services, rising with age and assessed under the COMPASS points framework. The S Pass requires S$3,300 in most sectors for the youngest applicants, rising to S$4,800 at age 45 and above. Both floors are scheduled to rise again from 1 January 2027.

These are floors set by MOM, not typical offer levels, so most incoming professionals land above them.

## Sending money back to Sri Lanka: remittance costs

Sending money home is a genuine recurring cost that generic cost-of-living guides skip entirely. As of mid-2026, one Singapore dollar buys roughly LKR 255 to 260, and the rupee has moved against the dollar over the year, so treat any specific rate as a snapshot rather than a fixed number.

Digital-first providers like Wise typically price close to the mid-market rate with a transparent, disclosed fee, and multiple comparison sites place Wise as the cheapest widely available option on the Singapore-to-Sri Lanka corridor as of 2026. Traditional bank wire transfers commonly carry a 5% to 8% markup on the exchange rate plus a flat wire fee, which is where most of the real cost hides.

Budget the remittance cost, the fee and spread rather than the transfer amount itself, as its own line item.

## Flights and staying connected to family

Round-trip flights between Singapore and Colombo take roughly 3 hours 45 minutes direct, with Singapore Airlines and SriLankan Airlines both flying the route, plus one-stop options via Malaysia AirAsia and Batik Air. Prices commonly range from roughly S$400 to S$650 round trip depending on season and booking lead time, with June typically cheaper and December running higher.

Budgeting one or two trips home a year, plus an occasional emergency fare, is a line item most generic cost-of-living guides leave out.

## Where Sri Lankan professionals and students tend to live

Little India and the Race Course Road area are an obvious first answer for anyone wanting groceries, Sri Lankan food, and an established community within walking distance. The Ceylon Sports Club on Balestier Road, a short distance away, is a longstanding gathering point for cricket, social events, and the wider Sri Lankan and Ceylonese diaspora, worth knowing about even if you don't live nearby.

For many working professionals, though, Little India isn't the most practical base once commute to the CBD, one-north, or a business park is factored in. Co-living rooms across the island put you within reach of Little India and Balestier without living there specifically, since MRT access makes a 15 to 20 minute trip routine. ## A realistic monthly budget for a Sri Lankan professional in Singapore

| Item | Typical monthly cost |
|---|---|
| Room rent (shared home, often all-inclusive) | S$800 to S$1,800 |
| Food, including Sri Lankan groceries | S$350 to S$600 |
| Transport (public transport, daily commute) | S$100 to S$150 |
| Phone and wifi (where not bundled into rent) | S$30 to S$60 |
| Remittance to Sri Lanka (fee and spread only, not the transfer amount itself) | S$15 to S$50 |
| Flights home (averaged monthly, 1 to 2 trips a year) | S$60 to S$110 |
| **Total, room + living costs** | **S$1,355 to S$2,770** |

This is a working professional's baseline, not a hard ceiling. Someone renting a whole unit alone instead of a room should add roughly S$1,000 to S$2,500 more to the rent line, since a full apartment costs considerably more and rarely includes utilities.

## Myth: Sri Lankan groceries make Singapore unaffordable

This gets said a lot, and it's overstated. Yes, specific imported ingredients like Ceylon spices and curry leaves cost more here than at home. But once you know Little India and the smaller Sri Lankan-run grocery outfits around it, that's one manageable line in a monthly budget, not the whole budget.

The bigger cost driver by far is rent, the same as it is for every nationality moving to Singapore. Anyone comparing headline "cost of living in Singapore" figures against Sri Lanka should be comparing rent-adjusted numbers, not grocery receipts, since rent is where the real gap sits.
