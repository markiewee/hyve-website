For a first three months in Singapore, co-living, a serviced apartment, and an extended-stay hotel are all workable choices, and the right one depends on budget, how much space and privacy you need, and whether you want to build any kind of routine while you house-hunt. Co-living tends to be the cheapest and the fastest way to meet people. A serviced apartment gives you the most private space and hotel-style service. A hotel gives you maximum flexibility to leave early but costs the most over three months and wasn't built for anyone to actually live in.

None of these is a wrong answer. Plenty of people land softly in each one. What matters is picking the one that matches what you're actually optimising for, not the one that sounds most familiar.

## Three Ways to Land Softly in Singapore

If you're new to the city, here's what each option actually is before we get into the comparison.

**Co-living** means renting a private room inside a shared house or apartment, with the kitchen and living room shared with other tenants. An operator manages the property, furniture, wifi, and utilities are usually bundled into one all-inclusive rent, and the minimum stay on private residential property in Singapore is three months. It's built for people staying weeks to a year, not decades.

**A serviced apartment** is a full self-contained unit, your own kitchen, living room, and bathroom, run by a management company that also handles housekeeping and sometimes a front desk. It's essentially a hotel room that grew into an apartment, aimed at business travellers and relocating executives who want space without signing a conventional lease.

**An extended-stay hotel** is a normal hotel room booked for weeks or months instead of nights. Daily housekeeping, no lease, and you can leave whenever you want. Nothing about the room itself was designed for long-term living, but the flexibility is real.

## What Each Option Costs in 2026

These are general ranges, not quotes, since actual prices move by neighbourhood, building age, and season.

**Co-living rooms** generally run from around S$1,000 at the low end up to S$2,500 or more for a central, ensuite room, all-inclusive of utilities, wifi, and common-area cleaning. **Serviced apartments** span a wide range, roughly S$3,000 to S$6,000 or more a month for a one-bedroom unit in a mainstream serviced residence, with premium branded buildings going well past that. Studio-style units can come in lower.

**Extended-stay hotels** are usually priced per night even when booked for months, and a budget room can still add up to somewhere in the region of S$1,600 to S$2,600 a month once multiplied out, sometimes more. A proper aparthotel with a kitchenette usually costs more than a basic room booked long-term.

The pattern that holds: co-living is generally the cheapest way to get a private room with real amenities included, a hotel is usually the most expensive per square foot for the least space, and a serviced apartment sits in between on price but gives you the most private square footage of the three.

| Typical monthly cost | Lowest of the three, all-inclusive | Mid to high, all-inclusive | Often highest once totalled, per-night pricing |
| Space | Private room, shared common areas | Full private unit | Single hotel room |

No option wins on every row, which is the actual point. A serviced apartment beats co-living on privacy and space. A hotel beats both on flexibility to leave whenever. Co-living beats both on cost and on giving you people to talk to in a new city.

## 6 Factors That Should Decide It For You

Instead of starting from which option sounds most professional or most familiar, work backwards from these six things.

**1. Your actual budget, not your ideal budget.** Work out what you can spend monthly, without stress, before you look at listings.

**2. How long you're actually staying.** If it's three months minimum and possibly longer, co-living and serviced apartments are both built for that. A hotel wasn't, and the cost gap widens the longer you stay.

**3. Whether you want to meet people or not.** A shared kitchen and living room in a co-living house puts you in the same space as other tenants daily. A serviced apartment or hotel puts you in a private box, fine if that's what you want, but it won't build a social circle on its own.

**4. How much you'll actually cook.** A shared kitchen (co-living) or a private one (serviced apartment) matters if you cook often. A hotel room usually has neither, which means eating out for three months straight.

**5. Whether you need total exit flexibility.** If there's a real chance your relocation falls through early, a hotel's nightly flexibility has genuine value even at a higher price. If you're confident you're staying, that flexibility is a feature you're paying for and not using.

**6. What "professional" actually means for your situation.** A lot of people default to a serviced apartment because it feels like the safe, corporate choice. Whether that's true for your situation is worth a closer look.

## Serviced Apartments Are the Professional Choice, Right?

This is one of the more common assumptions people bring into their first move, and it's not necessarily true.

Serviced apartments earned their reputation from decades of corporate relocation packages, where a company was paying and wanted a known, professional-grade product with a management company's name on the lease. That's a real strength, and for someone whose employer is footing the bill, a serviced apartment is a sound default.

But "professional" isn't the same as "best for me." A well-run co-living operator also has a lease, a managed property, included utilities, and someone responsible for fixing things. It's just a different structure, shared common space instead of a fully private unit, at a materially lower price. Plenty of working professionals choose co-living deliberately because the money saved is real and the shared living arrangement suits how they want to spend a first few months in a new city.

The honest version: a serviced apartment is professional in the sense of being polished and hands-off. Co-living is professional in the sense of being well-run and transparent. Neither is the "adult" choice with the other as compromise. It depends on what you're solving for.

## Temporary Housing in Singapore: A Beginner's Comparison

If you've never rented anywhere outside your home country, here's the plain version.

Singapore has a legal minimum stay of three months for renting private residential property, which covers most co-living rooms and many serviced apartments. Hotels aren't subject to that rule because you're booking accommodation, not signing a tenancy, which is exactly why a hotel can offer week-to-week flexibility that a lease-based option can't.

Co-living and serviced apartments both typically require some form of agreement and a deposit. A hotel usually just needs a credit card on file. That difference in paperwork is part of why a hotel can feel like the "easier" choice to book from overseas, even when it's not the better choice to actually live in for three months.

None of the three requires a Singapore bank account or local guarantor to get started, though each may have its own requirements around ID or deposit method, so check that before you book from abroad.

## Booking a Hotel for Three Months Because It Felt Safer

This is the mistake worth flagging directly, because it's a common one.

A hotel feels like the safe, no-commitment option when you're booking from another country and haven't seen anything in person. No lease, no deposit tied up for months, cancel any time. That instinct is understandable. It's also usually the most expensive way to solve a three-month problem, and it comes with real downsides that don't show up until you're actually living it.

A hotel room has no kitchen in most cases, which means three months of eating out. It has no shared space to meet anyone in, so if you don't already know people in Singapore, you're isolated by design. And because nightly hotel pricing doesn't get meaningfully cheaper for a long stay the way a monthly lease does, the total often ends up higher than a serviced apartment or co-living room over the same three months, for less space and less included.

The better version of "keep it flexible" is a co-living room or serviced apartment with a genuinely short minimum stay and a clear, published notice policy, not the assumption that a hotel is automatically cheaper or safer just because it doesn't ask you to commit. If you're unsure a booking is real before you're in the city, check who runs the property and whether pricing is published rather than gated behind an enquiry.

## Landing Options: The Questions Every Relocator Asks

**Is co-living cheaper than a serviced apartment in Singapore?**
Generally yes. Co-living rooms are typically priced lower per month, since you're renting a private room within a shared property rather than an entire self-contained unit.

**Can I book a hotel for three months instead of signing a lease?**
Yes, hotels aren't subject to the private residential minimum stay rule. It's usually the most expensive option of the three over a full three months, so weigh that against the flexibility you're actually going to use.

**What's the minimum stay for co-living in Singapore?**
Three months, for rooms on private residential property. This comes from Singapore's rules on renting private property, not from any individual operator's policy.

**Do serviced apartments have a minimum stay?**
It varies by operator and building. Some allow shorter stays than a standard lease, others set their own minimums. Check directly with the specific building.

**Which option is best for meeting people in a new city?**
Co-living, by a clear margin, since the shared kitchen and living room put you in regular contact with other tenants.

## Where This Leaves You

If cost and community matter most and you're settling in for three months or longer, co-living is worth a close look. If privacy and space are non-negotiable and the budget allows it, a serviced apartment does that well. If you genuinely might leave within days or need maximum flexibility above everything else, a hotel earns its higher price.
